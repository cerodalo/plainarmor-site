How to turn on DKIM in Microsoft 365

TL;DR: In the Microsoft Defender portal, open your domain's DKIM settings, copy the two CNAME values Microsoft gives you, publish them at your DNS host, wait for detection, then switch on "Sign messages for this domain with DKIM signatures." Do not copy values from examples; use the ones shown for your own domain.

What you are setting up

DKIM adds a digital signature to outgoing mail. Receivers look up your public key in DNS to check it. In Microsoft 365 the public keys are published through two CNAME records that point to Microsoft-hosted keys, so Microsoft can manage the keys for you.

Microsoft's documentation says that if you use only the initial onmicrosoft.com domain, signing is automatic. If you send as your own domain, such as example.com, you do the steps below.

What you need

  • Admin rights in Microsoft 365 to the Defender portal or Exchange Online.
  • Access to your DNS host to add CNAME records.
  • Your domain already added to Microsoft 365.

Portal menu names change. These steps follow Microsoft's current help page.

Step by step

1. Open the DKIM settings

In the Defender portal at security.microsoft.com, go to Email and collaboration, then Policies and rules, then Threat policies, then Email authentication settings. Select the DKIM tab.

2. Select your custom domain

Click the domain to open its details panel. If signing is not yet enabled, the panel shows the CNAME records you need under a heading about publishing CNAMEs, and may display an error stating that the records are missing. That is expected the first time. Microsoft's article says the error text includes the required values.

3. Copy the two records

You get two records, with host names selector1._domainkey and selector2._domainkey. Each points to a value specific to your domain. Microsoft's article shows these formats only as illustrations and tells you to use the portal or PowerShell for the real values.

If you prefer PowerShell, Microsoft documents this Exchange Online command, with your domain in place of example.com:

Get-DkimSigningConfig -Identity example.com | Format-List Name,Enabled,Status,Selector1CNAME,Selector2CNAME

4. Publish them at your DNS host

Add two CNAME records:

TypeNameValue
CNAMEselector1._domainkeyvalue shown for selector1
CNAMEselector2._domainkeyvalue shown for selector2

Paste values exactly. Do not add or remove trailing dots unless your host requires it. If your host appends your domain to the name, enter only selector1._domainkey.

5. Wait, then switch signing on

Microsoft says detection takes a few minutes or possibly longer. Return to the domain's panel and turn on the toggle labelled Sign messages for this domain with DKIM signatures. If it fails, wait and try again.

Verify it

Send a message from a Microsoft 365 address to a mailbox at another provider. In the original headers look for:

dkim=pass header.d=example.com

header.d should be your domain. If it shows your onmicrosoft.com name, signing for your custom domain is not active.

Common problems

  • Used values from an example. They only work for the example domain.
  • CNAME already exists. Edit it; do not add a duplicate.
  • DNS proxying. If your DNS host has a proxy option for records, turn it off for these CNAMEs so they resolve as plain DNS.
  • Wrong DNS host. The records must live where your nameservers point.
  • Other senders unsigned. Your newsletter and invoicing tools need their own DKIM; this only covers Microsoft 365 mail.

A hypothetical example

A small insurance agency at lakeshorecover.example runs Microsoft 365. Its administrator opens the DKIM tab, gets the two CNAME values, adds them at the DNS host, and waits an hour. The toggle then turns on cleanly. A test message shows dkim=pass for the agency's own domain. This scenario is invented.

Where DKIM fits

DKIM and SPF are the two checks DMARC uses. A DMARC policy of quarantine or reject needs at least one to pass and align. Having DKIM on your own domain keeps legitimate mail passing even when SPF breaks, for example when mail is forwarded. See our article on alignment for the details.

Key rotation

The portal includes a way to rotate DKIM keys. Microsoft's page notes this option is unavailable until signing is enabled. Routine rotation is optional for most small businesses; follow Microsoft's guidance if your security policy calls for it.

Several domains, one tenant

If your Microsoft 365 organization has more than one custom domain, each needs its own pair of CNAME records and its own signing toggle. Microsoft's documentation shows the example of an organization with two domains needing four CNAME records in total. The host names are the same on each domain, selector1._domainkey and selector2._domainkey, but the values differ, which is why you copy them from the portal for every domain separately.

For domains that do not send mail, skip DKIM and publish a no-mail SPF and a DMARC record instead.

Key takeaways

  • Custom domains need two CNAME records, selector1._domainkey and selector2._domainkey.
  • Get the exact values from the Defender portal or PowerShell, not from examples.
  • Wait for detection, then turn on the signing toggle.
  • Verify dkim=pass with your own domain in the headers.
  • Other sending tools need their own DKIM setup.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to do this for my onmicrosoft.com address?

No. Microsoft's documentation says outbound mail from the initial onmicrosoft.com domain is signed automatically. The steps here are for your own custom domain.

Why two CNAME records?

Microsoft uses two selectors, selector1 and selector2, so it can rotate keys without interrupting signing.

Can I copy the CNAME values from an article?

No. Microsoft says the values in its articles are illustrations. Get the exact values for your domain from the Defender portal or Exchange Online PowerShell.

How long until Microsoft detects the records?

Microsoft says it takes a few minutes or possibly longer to detect new CNAME records.
