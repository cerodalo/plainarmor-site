An email authentication checklist for small businesses

TL;DR: Work through six stages: know your domain, list your senders, set SPF and DKIM, publish DMARC at monitor, move to enforcement in steps, then lock down accounts and payment habits. Tick items off in order; skipping ahead is how legitimate mail gets blocked.

Stage 1: Know where you stand

  • [ ] Find where your domain's DNS is managed (where your nameservers point).
  • [ ] Confirm someone has a login to that DNS host and the login is not tied to a former employee.
  • [ ] Look up your DMARC record at _dmarc.yourdomain.com.
  • [ ] Look up your SPF record (TXT record starting v=spf1).
  • [ ] Note whether DKIM is enabled at your mailbox provider.

Our domain-check guide shows each lookup.

Stage 2: List every sender

  • [ ] Mailbox provider.
  • [ ] Invoicing, accounting and payment tools.
  • [ ] Newsletter and marketing platforms.
  • [ ] CRM, scheduling and reminder tools.
  • [ ] Website forms and online store.
  • [ ] Printers, scanners and alarm panels that email.
  • [ ] Anyone who sends "as you" on your behalf: agencies, bookkeepers, contractors.
  • [ ] Record an owner for each tool.

Stage 3: Set up SPF and DKIM

  • [ ] Exactly one SPF record at the root domain.
  • [ ] Each legitimate sender included or covered by DKIM.
  • [ ] No more than ten DNS lookups in SPF, per RFC 7208.
  • [ ] DKIM enabled at your mailbox provider, using your own domain.
  • [ ] DKIM enabled at each other sending tool, if it supports it.
  • [ ] Test message from each tool to an outside mailbox; check headers for dkim=pass or spf=pass aligned with your domain.

Provider-specific guides: SPF and DKIM for Google Workspace, and SPF and DKIM for Microsoft 365.

Stage 4: Publish DMARC at monitor

  • [ ] Create a mailbox or group for DMARC reports, or use a reporting service.
  • [ ] Publish a single DMARC record:
v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.com
  • [ ] Confirm it resolves with a lookup.
  • [ ] Schedule a review date two to four weeks out.

Google's own documentation recommends starting with p=none, as the policy to use when you begin.

Stage 5: Read reports and fix

  • [ ] Collect at least one full billing and newsletter cycle of reports.
  • [ ] Sort sources: passing, legitimate but failing, unknown.
  • [ ] Fix legitimate failures at each vendor.
  • [ ] Investigate unknown sources; some may be fakes.
  • [ ] Document the final sender list.

Stage 6: Move to enforcement in steps

  • [ ] p=quarantine with a low pct, then raise gradually.
  • [ ] Ask staff to report missing customer mail.
  • [ ] After a clean period, p=reject with a low pct, then raise.
  • [ ] Keep rua active.
  • [ ] Set sp= if you send from subdomains with different needs.
  • [ ] Park unused domains with v=spf1 -all and v=DMARC1; p=reject.

Our guide on moving from monitor to enforcement covers the pacing.

Stage 7: Beyond DMARC

DMARC protects your exact domain. These close other gaps:

  • [ ] Multi-factor authentication on every mailbox.
  • [ ] Review mailbox forwarding rules for anything unexpected.
  • [ ] Remove mailboxes for former staff.
  • [ ] Register a few obvious lookalike variants of your domain.
  • [ ] A written rule: payment or bank-detail changes are verified by phone, using a number you already had.
  • [ ] A line on invoices: "We never change bank details by email."
  • [ ] Two-person approval for payments above a limit you choose.
  • [ ] A named contact for staff to forward suspicious email.

Stage 8: Keep it alive

  • [ ] When you add a tool that sends email, authenticate it before launch.
  • [ ] Review DMARC reports monthly at first, then quarterly.
  • [ ] Re-check your records twice a year.
  • [ ] Keep the DNS login and the sender list in a safe shared place.

A hypothetical example of using this list

A small veterinary clinic (pawprint.example) works through the checklist. In Stage 2 it discovers an appointment reminder service nobody remembered. In Stage 5 the reports show it failing DKIM. The clinic sets up the vendor's DKIM records before enforcing. Without the list, that service's reminders would have been blocked. This scenario is invented.

What the checklist does not cover

It does not cover legal or regulatory compliance. If your industry has requirements, ask a qualified professional. It also cannot guarantee protection: receivers decide what to do with your policy, and attackers have routes your domain settings do not close.

Key takeaways

  • Follow the order: lookup, inventory, SPF and DKIM, DMARC monitor, then enforce.
  • One SPF record, one DMARC record.
  • Observe through a full billing cycle before enforcing.
  • Pair DNS settings with MFA and payment-verification habits.
  • Review whenever your tools change.

Frequently asked questions

How long does the whole checklist take?

Setup steps may take an afternoon, but observing reports and tightening policy safely takes weeks. Plan for a staged project, not a one-day task.

Who should own this?

One named person, such as the office manager or your IT contractor, with the owner's approval. DNS changes need someone who can log in to your DNS host.

Do I need all of it?

The first section is the minimum. The later sections reduce risk further but are not all required to begin.

How often should I revisit it?

Review whenever you add a tool that sends email, and at least twice a year.
