Lookalike domains vs. spoofed domains

TL;DR: A spoofed domain means the attacker fakes your exact domain in the From line. A lookalike domain means the attacker registers a different domain that resembles yours. DMARC addresses the first and does nothing for the second, so you need both technical settings and human habits.

Spoofed: your exact domain

Say your domain is maplewoodlaw.example. A spoofed message shows info@maplewoodlaw.example as the sender even though your servers never sent it. This is possible when your domain does not tell receivers to reject failures.

Because the domain is yours, you can fix it. Publishing SPF, DKIM and an enforced DMARC policy tells receivers how to treat mail that claims to be from your exact domain and fails checks.

Lookalike: a different domain

A lookalike is a real, separate domain that the attacker owns. Common patterns, using the same example:

PatternExample
Swapped or missing lettermaplewodlaw.example
Doubled lettermaplewoodlaww.example
Added wordmaplewoodlaw-billing.example
Hyphenmaple-woodlaw.example
Different endingmaplewoodlaw.co instead of maplewoodlaw.com
Lookalike charactersrn for m

The attacker can give that domain perfectly valid SPF, DKIM and DMARC records. Receivers see a properly authenticated message from a domain that just happens not to be yours. So authentication passes, and the message gets through.

Why attackers choose lookalikes

If your exact domain is protected, a lookalike is the next easiest step. It takes a few minutes and a small fee to register one. This is why enforcing DMARC is worth doing, but it is not the end of the story. It pushes attackers toward the other method, which is harder to block technically but easier for people to notice.

How to spot a lookalike

  1. Read the domain slowly, from right to left. The part just before the final ending is the real domain.
  2. Look for extra words like billing, secure, or support attached with a hyphen.
  3. Compare to an old email you know is real.
  4. Be wary of new addresses for people you already know.
  5. On phones, expand the sender.

What you can do

For your domain

  • Enforce DMARC so exact-domain faking is blocked.
  • Register obvious variants that you do not intend to use, such as common misspellings, and point them to nothing. You cannot cover every variant.
  • For any domains you own but do not send mail from, publish a record that says no mail should come from them:
Name:  example.org            Type: TXT   Value: v=spf1 -all
Name:  _dmarc.example.org     Type: TXT   Value: v=DMARC1; p=reject

(Use your own parked domains in place of the placeholder.)

For people

  • Make a rule that payment or bank changes are verified by phone.
  • Encourage customers to call you if an email looks odd.
  • Share your official billing address on your invoices so customers can compare.

For monitoring

Some services will watch for newly registered domains similar to yours. These can help but are not complete, and we are not recommending specific products here.

A hypothetical scenario

A bookkeeping firm, Cedar & Pine (cedarpine.example), has strong DMARC enforcement. An attacker registers cedarpine-accounts.example and writes to a client, using the firm's logo. DMARC for cedarpine.example has nothing to say about it. The client, who has been told to phone for any new account details, calls the firm and learns it is a fake. This is a made-up illustration.

If you find one

  • Save screenshots and the email headers.
  • Warn customers with a short plain message.
  • Ask the domain registrar or hosting provider to review it through their abuse reporting process.
  • Ask an attorney about trademark options if it keeps happening; we do not provide legal advice.

Talking to customers about lookalikes

You cannot control other people's domains, but you can prepare customers. A short note on your website or invoices works well:

"Our emails come only from addresses ending in @example.com. If a message appears to come from us but the address looks slightly different, please call us before acting on it."

It is also worth sharing the habit of reading the address from right to left. The last part is the real domain: in billing@example-payments.co, the domain is example-payments.co, not example. People who have heard this once are noticeably better at catching a lookalike, and it costs you nothing to say it.

Key takeaways

  • Spoofed means your exact domain; lookalike means a similar one the attacker owns.
  • DMARC helps with spoofed, not lookalike.
  • Lookalikes can pass authentication because the attacker controls their records.
  • Staff and customer habits are the main defense against lookalikes.
  • Park unused domains with a "no mail" policy.

Frequently asked questions

Does DMARC protect against lookalike domains?

No. A lookalike is a different domain that the attacker owns and can set up with its own valid records. Your DMARC policy only covers your own domain.

Should I register similar domains myself?

Registering a few obvious variants, such as common misspellings or other endings, can reduce easy abuse. You cannot register every possibility.

How do I find lookalikes of my domain?

Search domain-registration tools or monitoring services for close variants, and ask customers to forward anything odd. Keep expectations modest; coverage is never complete.

Is a lookalike illegal?

It can raise trademark or fraud issues depending on the facts. We cannot give legal advice; an attorney can explain your options.
