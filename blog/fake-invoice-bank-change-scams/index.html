Fake invoice and bank-detail-change scams: how they work and how to stop them

TL;DR: In a fake invoice or bank-change scam, someone emails a customer or vendor as you (or as them) and supplies different payment details. The best defense is a rule: never change payment details based on email alone. Verify by phone, and publish email authentication so your own domain is harder to fake.

How the scam works

There are two directions, and a small business can be on either end.

You are the payer. A message appears to come from a supplier: "Our bank has changed. Pay this invoice to the new account." You pay, the real supplier never gets the money, and weeks later they ask why the bill is unpaid.

You are the payee. A message appears to come from you. Your customer receives an invoice that looks right, with a bank account that is not yours. They pay the scammer. Then they blame you, and you have to sort it out.

The email is the vehicle. How it got to the inbox varies, as we cover below.

Three ways the fake arrives

  1. Spoofed From line. The scammer fakes your exact domain. This is possible when no enforced policy is published.
  2. Lookalike domain. A different domain with nearly the same spelling, like acme-billing.example instead of acme.example.
  3. Hacked mailbox. The attacker reads real threads, then replies in the right conversation with new details. This is the hardest to spot.

Each has a different defense, which is why no single tool solves it.

Warning signs

  • A change to bank details, especially with no warning.
  • A new account in a different bank or location than before.
  • Pressure to pay today, or a request to keep it quiet.
  • A reply-to address that differs from the sender.
  • Slightly different wording, tone or signature from the real contact.
  • A request to skip your normal approval step.

None of these proves fraud, but any of them is a reason to verify.

A payment-change routine that works

Write this down and make it the rule for everyone who touches money.

  1. Never act on an email alone when bank details change.
  2. Call a number you already had: from a past invoice, contract, or your records. Not the number in the new email.
  3. Ask a question only the real contact would know, such as the last project name.
  4. Get a second person to approve the first payment to any new account.
  5. Send a small test amount for large payments if your bank allows it, and confirm receipt before the rest.
  6. Write down the confirmation: who you spoke with and when.

This takes minutes. It is the most effective control because it works regardless of how the fake email arrived.

What to do on the payee side

If customers might be fooled by emails pretending to be you:

  • Add a line to every invoice: "We never change our bank details by email. If you receive such a message, call us."
  • Give customers a verified phone number on your website.
  • Publish SPF, DKIM and DMARC so receivers can reject mail that fakes your domain.
  • Consider monitoring for lookalike domains, because DMARC does not cover them.

The DMARC piece, briefly

DMARC lets your domain tell receivers what to do with messages that claim to be from you but fail checks. With a policy of quarantine or reject, supporting receivers send those messages to spam or refuse them. Because mail from a forgotten sender, such as your invoicing software, can also be affected, roll out in stages: monitor first, fix gaps, then enforce.

A starter record, with a placeholder domain and report address:

Name:  _dmarc.example.com
Type:  TXT
Value: v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.com

A hypothetical case

A small HVAC contractor, Summit Air (summitair.example), emails a property manager a monthly invoice. A stranger registers summitair-billing.example and writes: "Please use our new account." The property manager has a rule: any bank change gets a call. She calls the number on the original contract, and the contractor says no change was made. No money moves.

This is an illustration, not a real event. The point is that the habit worked even though the email looked right.

If money has already moved

  • Call your bank's fraud line immediately and ask about a recall.
  • Report to the FBI's IC3 at ic3.gov.
  • Tell the affected customer or vendor, with facts and no blame.
  • Preserve the emails, including the full headers.
  • Ask an attorney about your obligations; this article is not legal advice.

Making your own invoices harder to fake

Customers cannot tell a real invoice from a copy unless you give them something to compare against. A few small changes help:

  • Use the same sending address every time, and tell customers what it is.
  • Put a verification line in the footer: your phone number and a sentence that bank details never change by email.
  • Use a payment link or portal where customers pay through a page on your own website, so they never need to copy account numbers from a message.
  • Keep invoice numbering consistent, so a number out of sequence stands out.
  • Notify customers in advance if you really do change banks, by letter or phone, so an unexpected email is not the first they hear of it.

None of this needs special software, and all of it makes a fake easier to catch.

Key takeaways

  • Never change payment details based on email alone; verify by phone.
  • Scams can come from spoofed, lookalike, or hacked sources.
  • Add a "we never change bank details by email" line to invoices.
  • DMARC helps with exact-domain spoofing but is not a complete answer.
  • Roll DMARC out in stages to avoid blocking your own invoices.

Frequently asked questions

What is the quickest way to catch a fake bank change?

Call the vendor on a phone number you already had on file, not one from the email, and ask them to confirm the change.

Can DMARC stop fake invoices?

It can stop messages that fake your exact domain from reaching inboxes at receivers that enforce policy. It cannot stop a lookalike domain or a hacked mailbox, so payment verification is still needed.

Should I tell customers my bank details never change by email?

Yes. A short line in your invoices saying you never change bank details by email gives customers a simple rule to check against.

What if we already paid the wrong account?

Call your bank immediately and ask about a recall, then report it to the FBI's IC3 at ic3.gov. Speed matters. For legal questions, consult an attorney.
