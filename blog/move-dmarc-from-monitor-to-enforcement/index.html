How to move DMARC from monitor to enforcement safely

TL;DR: Publish p=none with reporting, find and fix every legitimate sender, then tighten in steps: quarantine for a small share of failing mail, then more, then reject. Each step waits for clean reports. Rushing is how businesses block their own invoices.

Why not just switch to reject?

p=reject asks receivers to refuse mail that fails DMARC. It is the strongest setting, and it is right for most domains eventually. But it does not know which of your senders are legitimate. If your invoicing tool, a website form or a newsletter service is not set up properly, its mail fails and is refused. Customers may never see it, and you may never be told.

A staged rollout trades speed for safety.

The stages

Stage 0: Get your basics right

  • One SPF record that lists your known senders.
  • DKIM turned on at your mailbox provider.
  • A DMARC record at p=none with a rua address that someone reads.
v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.com

Stage 1: Observe (2 to 4 weeks or more)

Collect aggregate reports. Cover at least one full billing cycle and one newsletter send. Sort sources into passing, legitimate but failing, and unknown. Our guide on finding your senders explains how.

Stage 2: Fix legitimate failures

For each legitimate sender that fails:

  • Turn on DKIM signing with your domain at that vendor (preferred).
  • Or add the vendor's documented SPF include.
  • Re-check after changes. Reports lag by a day or so.

Aim for a state where every legitimate source passes and aligns.

Stage 3: Quarantine, gradually

Change p=none to p=quarantine with a low pct:

v=DMARC1; p=quarantine; pct=10; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.com

Under RFC 7489, pct applies the policy to that share of failing mail, and the rest is treated with the next-lower policy. Receiver behavior varies, so treat the number as approximate. Raise it in steps, for example to 25, 50, then 100, waiting a week or more at each step and watching for complaints like "I did not get your invoice."

Stage 4: Reject, gradually

When quarantine at 100 is quiet, switch to reject:

v=DMARC1; p=reject; pct=10; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.com

Step up the same way. At the end:

v=DMARC1; p=reject; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.com

Stage 5: Maintain

  • Review reports monthly at first.
  • When you add a new tool that sends mail, authenticate it first.
  • Keep your sender list current.

Watch-outs

  • Subdomains. They inherit your policy unless you set sp=. Check any subdomain that sends mail.
  • Forwarding. SPF breaks when mail is forwarded. DKIM usually survives, which is another reason to have both.
  • Mailing lists. Messages sent through mailing lists can alter content and fail DKIM. Know whether your staff post to external lists.
  • Quarterly and annual senders. Tax-season mail or yearly renewals may not appear in short observation windows.
  • Reports not arriving. Not all receivers send them, but major mailbox providers generally do.

A hypothetical timeline

A ten-person engineering firm at ironbridge.example follows this path:

8pct=50

Timings are illustrative. Many firms need longer, and nothing here is a guarantee. This example is invented.

What to do if something breaks

  1. Check whether the affected message came from a sender you forgot.
  2. If urgent, step the policy back one level, and fix the sender.
  3. Contact the vendor for DKIM or SPF setup steps.
  4. Re-advance gradually.

Because DNS changes may take time to take effect, do not make several changes at once.

What enforcement does not do

It protects your exact domain from being faked at receivers that honor DMARC. It does not stop lookalike domains, and it does not prevent mailbox takeovers.

A short test routine before each step

Before you raise the policy, run the same quick checks every time:

  1. Send a message from each sending tool to a mailbox at a different provider.
  2. Open the original headers and confirm dmarc=pass.
  3. Check the latest reports for new failing sources.
  4. Ask one or two staff members whether anything has gone missing.
  5. Write down the date and the setting you are moving to.

After the change, repeat the first two checks within a day, and watch the next few days of reports. This routine takes about fifteen minutes and catches most problems while they are small.

Key takeaways

  • Start with p=none, observe, fix, then tighten.
  • Use pct to ease in, and wait between steps.
  • Check quarterly and seasonal senders before going to reject.
  • Keep a way back: you can loosen the policy if something breaks.
  • Maintain the setup when tools change.

Frequently asked questions

Can I go straight to p=reject?

You can, but it is risky. Any legitimate sender that fails authentication would have its mail refused. A staged approach catches those senders first.

What does pct do in a rollout?

It applies your policy to a percentage of failing mail. RFC 7489 defines it; teams use it to ease into quarantine or reject. Receiver behavior can vary.

How do I know I am ready for enforcement?

When your reports over several weeks show all legitimate senders passing and the remaining failures are unknown or unwanted sources.

Can I go back if something breaks?

Yes. Changing p back to none or quarantine is a DNS edit. Records can take time to propagate, so go slowly and monitor.
