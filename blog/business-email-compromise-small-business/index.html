Business email compromise (BEC): what small businesses need to know

TL;DR: Business email compromise is fraud that uses a believable email to trick someone into sending money or sensitive information. It works through trust, not malware. Basic habits, plus proper email authentication on your own domain, reduce the risk, though neither makes you immune.

What BEC means

The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) describes business email compromise as a scam that targets businesses and individuals who make legitimate transfers of funds. It is often carried out by compromising or imitating a legitimate email account through social engineering or computer intrusion. The FBI publishes its annual figures in the IC3 Internet Crime Report, and it lists BEC as one of the categories of reported losses. We do not quote the dollar totals here; read the current report at the source if you need exact figures.

In plain terms: someone sends a message that looks like it came from a person you trust, and asks you to do something with money.

Common BEC patterns

Fake invoice or changed bank details

A "vendor" says its bank account has changed and asks you to pay the next bill there. We cover this in detail in our guide to fake invoice scams.

Boss or executive request

A message that appears to come from the owner asks an employee to buy gift cards or send a wire quickly and quietly. See our guide on CEO impersonation.

Payroll redirect

A message from a "staff member" asks HR to change where their paycheck goes.

Real estate and closing payments

A message imitates a title company or agent and gives new wiring instructions. Hypothetical, but a pattern the FBI has warned about in general terms.

Data requests

Someone posing as an executive asks for employee tax forms or a client list.

Three ways the email gets in front of you

  1. A hacked mailbox. The attacker logs in as a real person and writes from the real account.
  2. A spoofed address. The attacker fakes the From line of your domain or a partner's.
  3. A lookalike domain. The attacker registers a name that looks nearly identical.

The defenses differ. Strong sign-in (multi-factor authentication) addresses the first. Email authentication with an enforced DMARC policy helps with the second. Staff awareness and a watchful eye on names help with the third.

A hypothetical walk-through

Suppose a landscaping company, Greenline Landscaping (greenline.example), pays a supplier every month. One Tuesday, the bookkeeper receives a message from what looks like the supplier's address: "We've updated our bank. Please use the new details attached for this month's payment."

A good routine stops this. The bookkeeper calls the supplier at the number already on file, not the number in the email, and asks whether the change is real. It takes two minutes. The rule is simple: no bank detail change is accepted from an email alone.

This is a made-up example to show the routine, not a real case.

What small businesses can do

Process controls (the biggest wins)

  • Verify any payment change by calling a known number.
  • Require two people for payments above an amount you choose.
  • Treat urgency and secrecy as warning signs, not reasons to hurry.
  • Keep a list of vendor contacts outside email.

Account controls

  • Turn on multi-factor authentication for every mailbox.
  • Review mailbox forwarding rules; attackers sometimes add a hidden rule.
  • Remove old accounts of former staff.

Domain controls

  • Publish SPF and DKIM for every service that sends as you.
  • Publish DMARC, starting with p=none for reports, then move to quarantine and reject in stages.

Start with monitoring because a strict policy can block legitimate mail from a forgotten sender, such as your invoicing system.

What to do if you suspect a scam

If money was sent, contact your bank right away and ask about recalling the transfer. Then file a report at the FBI's IC3 site (ic3.gov). Speed matters, and the FBI's guidance emphasizes reporting quickly. This is general information, not legal advice; ask your bank and an attorney about your specific situation.

Building a simple habit that scales

Policies fail when they are long. A short script that everyone remembers works better than a binder nobody reads.

Try these three sentences, printed and taped near the accounting desk:

  1. "If money or login details are involved, I confirm by phone."
  2. "I use a number I already had, not one from the email."
  3. "If I feel rushed, that is the signal to slow down."

Owners can reinforce it by thanking staff when they stop to check, even if the message turns out to be real. People who get criticized for double-checking quickly stop doing it. People who are thanked keep doing it, and that is the behavior that stops these scams.

It also helps to decide in advance who may approve a bank-detail change, and to keep a short list of approved payees. Then "a new account" becomes an event that triggers a check, rather than something that slips through on a busy afternoon.

Key takeaways

  • BEC is fraud built on a believable email, not malware.
  • It can come from hacked accounts, spoofed addresses, or lookalike domains.
  • Verifying payment changes by phone stops most of the pattern's power.
  • DMARC covers exact-domain spoofing only; it does not cover the other two routes.
  • Report suspected fraud quickly to your bank and to IC3.

Frequently asked questions

What does BEC stand for?

Business email compromise. The FBI also uses the term email account compromise (EAC) for similar scams aimed at individuals.

Is BEC always caused by a hacked mailbox?

No. Some cases involve real account takeover, others use spoofed addresses or lookalike domains. The common thread is a convincing request that pushes someone to move money or share data.

Are small businesses really targeted?

Any organization that pays invoices or moves money by email can be. Smaller teams often have fewer second checks, which scammers may count on.

What is the single best defense?

Confirm any change to payment details through a second channel you already trust, such as a known phone number, before sending money.
