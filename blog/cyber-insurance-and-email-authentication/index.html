Cyber insurance and email authentication: what small businesses should know

TL;DR: Cyber insurance applications sometimes include questions about email security, such as multi-factor authentication, filtering, and whether SPF, DKIM and DMARC are set up. Requirements differ by insurer, and we have no authoritative source on any particular one, so use this as a prompt for questions to ask your agent, not as a statement of what insurers require.

This is general information, not insurance or legal advice.

Why the topic comes up

Business email compromise is a common route for fraud against organizations. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center describes it as a scam targeting businesses and individuals who make legitimate funds transfers. Insurers who cover cyber and fraud losses have a reason to care about the controls that make impersonation harder.

We could not find an authoritative, insurer-neutral source stating that carriers require DMARC, so we do not claim that they do. What we can say is that applications vary and that email-related questions are plausible.

Questions you may see

On cyber insurance applications or renewal forms, you may be asked things like:

  • Is multi-factor authentication required for email access?
  • Do you filter inbound email for spam, phishing and malicious attachments?
  • Do you have SPF, DKIM or DMARC configured for your domain?
  • Do you have a process to verify changes to payment instructions?
  • Do staff receive security awareness training?
  • Do you have a written procedure for funds transfers?

These are examples of the type of question, not a quote from any form.

Why accuracy matters

Whatever you answer should be true on the day you sign. If a form asks whether DMARC is enforced, "yes" should mean your record is at quarantine or reject, not none. You can check with a DNS lookup of _dmarc.yourdomain.com. Our domain-check guide shows how.

If you are unsure, ask your IT contractor to confirm, or say so to your agent. Do not guess on an application.

Controls that help, whatever the form says

  • Multi-factor authentication on every mailbox. Prevents many account takeovers.
  • SPF, DKIM and DMARC enforcement. Addresses faking of your exact domain.
  • Payment-change verification. Calling a known number before changing bank details.
  • Two-person approval for payments above a threshold.
  • Staff training on impersonation emails.
  • Mailbox rule reviews for hidden forwarding.

These are good practice regardless of insurance.

What email authentication does not change

  • It does not guarantee a claim will be paid.
  • It does not guarantee a lower premium.
  • It does not cover lookalike domains or hacked mailboxes.
  • It does not replace reading your policy.

Questions for your agent

  1. Which email-related controls does my application ask about, and how are answers verified?
  2. Does my policy cover funds-transfer fraud or social engineering? What are the limits, sub-limits and conditions?
  3. Are there conditions such as call-back verification that must be followed for coverage?
  4. What are the notice requirements if I suspect a fake invoice or account compromise?
  5. If I improve my controls mid-term, should I tell you, and could it affect my renewal?

Write down the answers and keep them with the policy.

A hypothetical example

A small engineering firm at redcedar.example is renewing a cyber policy. The renewal form asks whether DMARC is in place. The office manager checks and sees p=none. Rather than answering yes, she tells the agent the truth, begins a staged move toward quarantine, and asks whether progress on the project matters at renewal. She also confirms what the policy says about funds-transfer fraud. This scenario is invented.

What to do now

  1. Look up your DMARC record and note its policy.
  2. Turn on multi-factor sign-in for all mailboxes.
  3. Write down your payment-change verification rule.
  4. Read the social-engineering or funds-transfer section of your policy.
  5. Bring questions to your agent before renewal, not after an incident.

Keeping evidence you can show

Whatever an insurer asks, being able to show what you did is useful. Keep a simple folder, in a place that is not just email, with:

  • A dated screenshot or text copy of your DMARC, SPF and DKIM records each time you change them.
  • A note of who made each change and why.
  • Your written payment-verification rule and the date it was adopted.
  • The date multi-factor authentication was enabled on mailboxes.
  • A list of tools that send email as your domain.
  • Your policy documents and your agent's contact details.

This is ordinary good housekeeping, and it also makes any later conversation with an agent, an auditor or an IT contractor faster. It does not guarantee any outcome, and it is not a substitute for reading your policy.

Key takeaways

  • Insurers vary; ask your own agent what applies to you.
  • Answer application questions truthfully; p=none is not enforcement.
  • Controls like MFA and call-back verification are worth having regardless.
  • Check your policy for funds-transfer and social-engineering coverage and conditions.
  • Know your notice requirements before an incident.

Frequently asked questions

Does my insurer require DMARC?

It varies by insurer and policy. Some applications ask about email security controls. Check your own application and ask your agent, because we cannot speak for any specific insurer.

Does having DMARC lower my premium?

We cannot say. Pricing is set by each insurer. Ask your agent whether specific controls affect underwriting or terms.

Are fake-invoice losses covered?

Coverage for funds-transfer or social-engineering losses differs by policy, and may have sub-limits or conditions. Read the wording and ask your agent.

Should I tell my insurer about a fake email incident?

Review your policy's notice requirements and ask your agent promptly. Timing and conditions can matter.
