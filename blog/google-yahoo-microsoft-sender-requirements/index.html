Google, Yahoo and Microsoft email sender requirements: what small businesses need to know

TL;DR: Google says its sender requirements for bulk senders (5,000 or more messages a day to Gmail) took effect February 1, 2024, and include SPF, DKIM, DMARC, and one-click unsubscribe for marketing mail. Yahoo published similar requirements, and Microsoft announced rules for high-volume senders to Outlook.com. Small senders should still authenticate, because all senders to Gmail need at least SPF or DKIM.

Rules change. Read the provider pages linked in Sources for the current terms.

Google (Gmail)

According to Google's email sender guidelines page:

  • Effective date for the bulk-sender requirements: February 1, 2024.
  • All senders: need SPF or DKIM authentication for the domain.
  • Bulk senders (the page refers to senders of 5,000 or more messages per day): need SPF and DKIM, and must set up DMARC for the sending domain. Google states the DMARC enforcement policy can be set to none.
  • Alignment: for direct email, the domain in the From header must align with either the SPF domain or the DKIM domain.
  • Spam rate: Google tells senders to keep it below 0.30 percent, as measured in Postmaster Tools.
  • Marketing and subscribed messages: must support one-click unsubscribe and include a clearly visible unsubscribe link in the message body.
  • Other technical items: valid forward and reverse DNS (PTR) records, a TLS connection, and messages formatted to the Internet Message Format standard (RFC 5322).

Yahoo

Yahoo's Sender Hub best-practices page describes requirements taking effect in February 2024:

  • All senders: SPF or DKIM at a minimum, spam complaint rates below 0.3 percent, valid forward and reverse DNS records, and compliance with the SMTP and message-format standards.
  • Bulk senders: both SPF and DKIM, a DMARC policy of at least p=none with the From domain aligned to SPF or DKIM, one-click unsubscribe support with a visible unsubscribe link, and honoring unsubscribe requests within two days.
  • Yahoo also advises sending only to people who opted in, and mentions CAN-SPAM compliance.

Microsoft (Outlook.com)

Microsoft published a Defender for Office 365 blog post titled "Strengthening Email Ecosystem: Outlook's New Requirements for High-Volume Senders." Public summaries of that announcement describe requirements for senders of more than 5,000 emails per day to Outlook.com, Hotmail.com and Live.com, covering SPF, DKIM and a DMARC policy of at least p=none, with enforcement beginning in May 2025 and non-compliant mail going to Junk and potentially being rejected later.

We could not retrieve the full text of Microsoft's post when preparing this article, so treat those specifics as secondary-source summaries and confirm the exact thresholds, dates and consequences on Microsoft's page.

What these rules have in common

  • Authenticate with SPF and DKIM, using your own domain.
  • Publish a DMARC record. A policy of none meets the DMARC requirement stated by Google and Yahoo.
  • Make sure the domain in the From address aligns with SPF or DKIM.
  • Keep complaints low and honor unsubscribes for marketing mail.

What they mean for a small business

If you send a few hundred emails a day

You are probably below the bulk threshold. Still, Google and Yahoo both say all senders need SPF or DKIM, and authentication helps your normal mail be trusted. Setting these up is good practice regardless of volume.

If you send newsletters or promotions

Volume to one provider can add up, especially if your list includes many Gmail, Yahoo and Outlook addresses. If you might be near a threshold, make sure the tool sending the mail authenticates with your domain, supports one-click unsubscribe, and is configured so that DMARC aligns.

If you use a third-party tool

Most mainstream tools support these requirements, but you must complete their domain authentication step. Mail that "passes" only for the vendor's domain will not align with yours.

What these rules do not do

They are about the sender's compliance and deliverability. A p=none policy satisfies the DMARC requirement, but it does not ask receivers to block fakes of your domain. For that, you need quarantine or reject. See our guide on p=none.

A hypothetical example

A small online retailer at harvestmoon.example sends a weekly promotion to a large list. It checks Google's guidelines, finds that its newsletter tool signs with the vendor's domain only, and sets up custom DKIM so DMARC aligns. It also confirms the unsubscribe link works in one click. The retailer publishes p=none first, then plans enforcement. This scenario is invented.

A short action list

  1. Confirm SPF and DKIM for every sending tool.
  2. Publish DMARC with reports.
  3. Test that alignment passes.
  4. Check unsubscribe handling for marketing mail.
  5. Monitor spam complaint rates in Google Postmaster Tools if you send in volume.
  6. Re-read the providers' pages every so often.

Key takeaways

  • Google's bulk-sender requirements took effect February 1, 2024, for senders of 5,000 or more messages a day.
  • Google and Yahoo both allow DMARC p=none to satisfy the DMARC requirement for bulk senders.
  • Microsoft announced similar high-volume rules for Outlook.com; verify its details at the source.
  • Everyone should authenticate with SPF or DKIM.
  • Meeting these rules is not the same as blocking spoofing; enforcement is a separate step.

Frequently asked questions

Do these rules apply to a small business that sends a few emails a day?

The DMARC requirements are aimed at bulk senders, defined by Google as 5,000 or more messages a day. Google's guidelines say all senders need SPF or DKIM, so even small senders should authenticate.

Do I need p=reject to meet them?

No. Google's guidelines say a bulk sender's DMARC policy can be set to none. A policy of none still counts as having DMARC for that rule.

What happens if I do not comply?

Receivers may filter or reject non-compliant mail. Exact enforcement is described in each provider's documentation and may change, so read the current pages.

Where can I read the official rules?

Google's email sender guidelines, Yahoo's Sender Hub best practices, and Microsoft's Defender for Office 365 blog post on Outlook's requirements for high-volume senders.
