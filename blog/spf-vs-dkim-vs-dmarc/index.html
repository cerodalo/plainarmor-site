SPF vs DKIM vs DMARC: the plain-English difference

TL;DR: SPF is a list of servers allowed to send for your domain. DKIM is a signature that proves a message was signed by your domain and was not altered. DMARC ties them to the From address people see, tells receivers what to do with failures, and sends you reports.

An analogy: the mailroom

Picture a company mailroom that handles outgoing letters.

  • SPF is the approved-couriers list. It says, "Only these delivery trucks may carry our letters."
  • DKIM is the wax seal. Each letter carries a seal that only the company can make. If someone opens and changes the letter, the seal breaks.
  • DMARC is the policy poster by the door. It says, "A letter must have a valid truck or a valid seal, and it must match the name on the letterhead. If not, here is what to do with it. And please send us a weekly report."

Each does a different job. None replaces the others.

SPF in a bit more detail

SPF (RFC 7208) is a TXT record at your domain. It names the servers or services allowed to send mail with your domain in the hidden "envelope" return address.

v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com ~all

Strengths: easy to publish, widely understood.

Limits:

  • It checks the hidden envelope address, not the From line people read.
  • It can break when mail is forwarded.
  • It is limited to ten DNS lookups.
  • It lists sources but does not itself say how receivers must act, beyond an ending such as ~all or -all.

DKIM in a bit more detail

DKIM (RFC 6376) adds a cryptographic signature to a message's headers. The receiver finds your public key in DNS (at a name like selector._domainkey.example.com) and checks the signature.

Strengths: survives most forwarding; shows the message was not modified in transit.

Limits:

  • Each sending service must be set up to sign with your domain.
  • A signature from the vendor's own domain does not help your DMARC.
  • Mailing lists that modify messages can break it.

DMARC in a bit more detail

DMARC (RFC 7489) is a TXT record at _dmarc.yourdomain.com. It does three things:

  1. Alignment. It requires that SPF or DKIM pass for a domain that matches the visible From domain.
  2. Policy. It tells receivers what you want for failures: none, quarantine or reject.
  3. Reporting. It asks receivers to send aggregate reports to you.
v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.com

How they work together

When a receiver gets a message that says it is from example.com:

  1. It checks SPF: was the sending server allowed?
  2. It checks DKIM: is there a valid signature?
  3. It looks up DMARC: did either pass in alignment with the From domain? If not, what is the policy?
  4. It applies your policy, according to its own rules, and may send you a report.

Under RFC 7489, a message passes DMARC when at least one of SPF or DKIM passes with a domain that aligns with the From domain.

A hypothetical example

A small realty office at oakhollow.example has SPF listing Google, and DKIM on. A stranger sends mail with agent@oakhollow.example in the From line from an unknown server. SPF fails (not an approved sender). There is no DKIM signature. DMARC fails. If the office's policy is p=none, the receiver is not asked to act. If it is p=reject, supporting receivers are asked to refuse the message. This scenario is invented.

Common confusion

  • "I have SPF, so I'm protected." SPF alone does not tell receivers what to do, and it does not check the From line people see.
  • "DKIM means the email is trustworthy." It means the signing domain is verified, not that the content is honest. A scammer can sign mail from their own lookalike domain.
  • "DMARC will fix my spam problem." DMARC concerns messages that claim to be from your domain. It does not filter spam coming to you.

What to do

  1. Set up SPF and DKIM for each service that sends as you.
  2. Publish DMARC at p=none with reports.
  3. Move to enforcement in stages.

Key takeaways

  • SPF = approved senders; DKIM = signature; DMARC = policy plus reporting.
  • DMARC needs SPF or DKIM to pass in alignment with the From domain.
  • DKIM usually survives forwarding; SPF often does not.
  • None of them stop lookalike domains or hacked mailboxes.
  • Set up all three, then enforce DMARC gradually.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need all three?

Yes, for the best result. DMARC depends on SPF and DKIM, and SPF and DKIM alone do not tell receivers what to do with failures.

Which one matters most?

DMARC is the one that sets policy and sends reports, but it only works if SPF or DKIM passes for your legitimate mail.

Are they DNS records?

Yes. SPF and DMARC are TXT records. DKIM public keys are published as TXT or CNAME records, depending on the provider.

Does having all three stop all fake email?

No. It helps against faking your exact domain. Lookalike domains and hacked mailboxes are separate problems.
