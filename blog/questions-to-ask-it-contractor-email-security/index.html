What to ask your IT contractor about email security

By The PlainArmor Team · Last reviewed October 3, 2026 · 6 min read

TL;DR: Ask your IT contractor what your DMARC policy is today, who reads the reports, which tools send mail as you, whether multi-factor sign-in is on for every mailbox, and who holds the DNS login. Specific, checkable answers are a good sign. Vague reassurance is a reason to follow up.

Why ask

Email security often falls between roles. The owner assumes IT handles it. IT assumes nothing is wrong because mail works. Meanwhile, the domain's DNS might have no policy at all. These questions bring the details into the open, in a friendly way.

The questions

About your domain's settings

1. What is our DMARC policy right now: none, quarantine, or reject? Good answer: states the policy and when it was set. Follow up if it is none: "What is the plan and timeline to move up?"

2. Who receives the DMARC reports, and who reads them? Good answer: a named person or a monitoring service, with a regular review. A report address no one checks is a common gap.

3. Which services send email as our domain, and are all of them authenticated? Good answer: a written list of tools with SPF or DKIM status. If no list exists, ask for one.

4. Is there exactly one SPF record, and are we under the lookup limit? Good answer: yes to both, and they know why it matters. RFC 7208 allows a limited number of DNS lookups during SPF evaluation.

5. Is DKIM enabled with our own domain at each sending service? Good answer: names the services and confirms testing of headers.

About access and accounts

6. Is multi-factor authentication required on every mailbox, including shared and admin accounts? Good answer: yes, with any exceptions explained.

7. Who holds the DNS login, and is it stored somewhere we can reach? Good answer: at least two trusted people have access, and ownership of the domain registration is in the company's name. If the contractor controls the only login, plan to change that.

8. How do we review mailbox forwarding rules and sign-in alerts? Good answer: a routine or tool, not "we would see it if it happened."

9. What happens to a mailbox when someone leaves? Good answer: a checklist that includes disabling sign-in and reviewing rules.

About process and response

10. What is our process if a customer reports a fake email from us? Good answer: a short plan: collect the original message, check headers, check account activity, notify affected customers, report. See our guide on what to do.

11. How do we verify bank-detail changes and wire requests? Good answer: a call to a known number and a second approver. This is a process, not a tool, and the contractor can help you write it down.

12. What does our setup not cover? Good answer: an honest list, such as lookalike domains and compromised mailboxes, and what is done about each. Beware of anyone who says "we are fully protected."

How to use the answers

Make a one-page summary:

ItemAnswerDate checked
DMARC policy
Report reader
Sender list
MFA coverage
DNS login holders
Incident plan

Review it twice a year.

You can verify some answers yourself

A DNS lookup is public and free. Check _dmarc.yourdomain.com and your SPF record using a lookup tool, as in our domain-check guide. Presenting the result as a friendly "I looked this up; can we walk through it?" works better than a challenge.

Warning signs

  • "Email just works, so it is fine."
  • A DMARC record that was set years ago and never reviewed.
  • No list of sending tools.
  • The contractor holds the only DNS login and will not share it.
  • Promises of complete protection.
  • Advice to jump to p=reject immediately without observation.

A hypothetical example

An owner of a small accounting practice at maplecrest.example asks question 1 and learns the policy is none, set up years ago. Question 2 reveals that reports go to a former employee's mailbox. Nothing alarming, but it shows why asking matters. The contractor and owner agree on a reporting address, a sender list, and a staged plan. This scenario is invented.

What if your contractor is not the right fit

Some providers focus on devices and support, not email authentication. That is fine. You can add a specialist for the DNS work or follow a written plan yourself. The key is that someone owns it.

How to start the conversation

Many owners hesitate because they do not want to seem to doubt their provider. A friendly opening helps:

"I was reading about how fake emails can use a company's own domain name. Could we spend fifteen minutes going through our email settings together? I would like to understand where we stand."

Then take notes, ask for anything promised in writing, and set a date to review again. Good contractors tend to welcome these conversations, since a clear list of senders and a documented policy makes their own work easier.

Key takeaways

  • Ask for the current DMARC policy, report reader, and sender list.
  • Confirm MFA, DNS login access and offboarding.
  • Ask what the setup does not cover.
  • Verify what you can with public lookups.
  • Treat vague answers as a prompt to follow up, not a reason to blame.

Put your own domain to the test. Enter your work email on the home page and nothing is sent to anyone.

Frequently asked questions

I am not technical. Can I still ask these?

Yes. The questions are written in plain language, and a good provider can answer them without jargon. If answers are vague, ask for them in writing.

Should I check their answers?

You can verify several yourself with public lookups, such as your DMARC and SPF records. That is a fair, friendly check.

What if my contractor says it is all handled?

Ask what the DMARC policy is, who reads the reports, and when it was last reviewed. Specific answers are a good sign.

Who should have the DNS login?

At least two trusted people, with the login stored somewhere safe, so that you are not dependent on one person or vendor.
