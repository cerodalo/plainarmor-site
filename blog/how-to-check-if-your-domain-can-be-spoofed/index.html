How to check if your domain can be spoofed

TL;DR: Look up the DMARC TXT record at _dmarc.yourdomain.com. If there is none, or its policy is p=none, your domain is not telling receivers to block fakes. Then confirm SPF and DKIM exist for your real senders. This takes about ten minutes and needs no login.

What you are checking

Three public settings decide whether fakes of your exact domain are blocked:

  1. DMARC, the policy, which is the main one.
  2. SPF, a list of servers allowed to send for your domain.
  3. DKIM, a signature your real senders add.

All three are DNS TXT or CNAME records. Anyone can read them, including you.

Step 1: Find your DMARC record

Use a free DMARC lookup website, or open a command window and type (replace example.com with your domain):

nslookup -type=TXT _dmarc.example.com

Results fall into three groups.

What you seeMeaning
No record, or "non-existent domain"No policy. Fakes are not addressed.
v=DMARC1; p=none; ...Monitoring only. Does not ask receivers to block.
v=DMARC1; p=quarantine; ... or p=rejectEnforcement. This is the protective setting.

Step 2: Check the policy details

Even when you see quarantine or reject, read the rest of the record:

  • pct= is the portion of mail the policy applies to. Under RFC 7489 the default is all of it. A low pct means most failing mail is treated as p=none would treat it. Newer practice is evolving, so read it as "partial enforcement".
  • sp= is the policy for subdomains. If missing, subdomains use p.
  • rua= is where reports go. Is it an address someone actually reads?

Our guide on reading your DMARC record goes through each tag.

Step 3: Check SPF

Look up your domain's TXT records and find the one that starts with v=spf1:

nslookup -type=TXT example.com

Healthy signs:

  • There is exactly one SPF record. Two records cause failures.
  • It names your real senders, for example your mailbox provider.
  • It ends with ~all or -all, not +all, which would allow anyone.

A typical Google Workspace example looks like this:

v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com ~all

Step 4: Check DKIM

DKIM records live under a "selector," which differs by provider. Common examples include google._domainkey for Google Workspace and selector1._domainkey for Microsoft 365. Query it:

nslookup -type=TXT google._domainkey.example.com

If your provider uses CNAME records (Microsoft 365 does), query CNAME instead. If nothing shows, DKIM may not be enabled for your domain. Our DKIM guides for Google Workspace and Microsoft 365 explain turning it on.

Step 5: Find every sender

The hardest part is not the records but the senders. List every service that sends mail as you: your mailbox provider, invoicing or accounting software, online store, newsletter tool, CRM, website forms, and printers or scanners. Any of them that is missing from your SPF or does not sign with DKIM will fail DMARC once you enforce it.

Reading your result

No DMARC record. Fakes are not addressed. Start with a monitor-only record.

p=none. Good start, no blocking. Use the reports to find senders, then move up.

p=quarantine. Failing mail goes to spam at supporting receivers.

p=reject. Failing mail is refused at supporting receivers. Make sure legitimate mail is passing first.

What this check cannot tell you

  • Whether lookalike domains exist.
  • Whether a mailbox is hacked.
  • Whether a particular receiver will honor your policy. Receivers decide, and results vary.

A hypothetical result

A small florist at petalworks.example looks up _dmarc.petalworks.example and finds nothing. It sends invoices through accounting software and newsletters through a bulk-email service, plus regular mail through a mailbox provider. That is three senders, and the florist cannot say which pass authentication. The sensible next step is a p=none record with a reporting address, then reading the reports for a few weeks. This is an invented scenario.

Interpreting odd results

Real-world lookups are sometimes messier than the tidy examples above. A few things you might see:

  • A DMARC record on a subdomain but not the main domain. The main domain still lacks a policy. Check _dmarc.yourdomain.com specifically.
  • A record with a rua pointing to a mailbox that does not exist. The policy still works, but reports are lost.
  • Two SPF records. This is an error; merge them.
  • An SPF ending in +all. This effectively allows any server and should be corrected.
  • A CNAME at _dmarc. Some setups point _dmarc at another domain's record. That can be valid, but make sure you understand where it leads.
  • Different answers from different tools. DNS caching can cause this for a short time after a change. Wait and try again.

If any result confuses you, save it and ask your IT contact or DNS host support. Showing them the exact output saves time.

Key takeaways

  • Look up _dmarc.yourdomain first; no record or p=none means no blocking.
  • Check there is one SPF record that covers your senders.
  • Check DKIM is enabled at each sending service.
  • List every sender before tightening policy.
  • DNS lookups are public and harmless; no login needed.

Frequently asked questions

Can I check without special software?

Yes. A free online DMARC or DNS lookup tool, or the nslookup command on your computer, can show your records. No login to your DNS account is needed.

Is it safe to look up my own DNS records?

Yes. DNS records are public by design. Looking them up changes nothing.

What if my result says p=none?

It means you are monitoring only. Fake messages are not asked to be blocked. Plan a staged move to quarantine and then reject.

Should I send a test fake email to myself?

Not required, and we do not recommend impersonating your own domain with tools you do not control. The DNS records tell you what you need.
