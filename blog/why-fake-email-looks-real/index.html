Why your email looks real when it is fake

TL;DR: A fake email can look perfect because everything you see, including the name, address, logo and signature, is content the sender controls. The only reliable evidence of who sent a message is in its technical headers, and those only help if the sending domain publishes and enforces authentication.

What you see versus what is true

When you open an email you see the sender's name, an address, the subject, and the body. All of that is data the sender typed or attached. The logo is a picture file anyone can save from your website. The signature is text anyone can copy.

It is like a business card. A printed card tells you what the person wants you to believe. It does not tell you whether they work there.

The parts a scammer can fake easily

  • Display name. The text beside the address, such as "Jordan at Acme". Free to set to anything.
  • From address. Fakeable unless the domain's policy catches it.
  • Reply-To. Can silently send your reply somewhere else.
  • Logo, colors, footer. Copied from public sources.
  • Writing style. Easier now, because anyone can read a few real emails and imitate them.
  • Thread history. In a hacked-mailbox case, the attacker may reply inside a genuine conversation.

The parts that are harder to fake

The technical headers contain results from checks the receiving server ran. A message might show something like:

Authentication-Results: mx.example.net;
  spf=fail smtp.mailfrom=example.org;
  dkim=none;
  dmarc=fail header.from=yourcompany.example

In plain English: SPF failed, there was no DKIM signature, and DMARC failed for the domain in the From line. A mail provider may then deliver it to spam or block it, depending on the sending domain's policy and its own rules. This is a made-up header for illustration.

You can find this by choosing "Show original" in Gmail, or "View message source" or "View message details" in other clients. Labels vary, so search your app's help.

Why checks do not always save you

Receivers can only apply the rules the domain owner has published. A domain with no DMARC record leaves each receiver to guess. A domain at p=none explicitly says "take no action." In both cases a failing message may still arrive.

When a domain publishes p=reject, supporting receivers are asked to refuse failing messages. This is the intended purpose of DMARC under RFC 7489.

Why display names are even trickier on phones

Phone mail apps often show only the display name and hide the address. A message from sam.larkspur@random-mail.example can display as "Sam Larkspur" and look just like the boss. Tapping the name usually reveals the full address.

A simple look-before-you-act checklist

  1. Expand the sender and read the full address.
  2. Compare the domain letter by letter with the real one.
  3. Hover (or long-press) any link to see where it goes before you click.
  4. Check whether the request involves money, passwords or data. If so, confirm by phone.
  5. Look at whether the Reply-To differs from the From.
  6. If unsure, forward the message to your IT contact rather than answering.

What you can do about your own domain

You cannot control what other people's inboxes display, but you can control what your domain says about itself:

  1. Publish SPF listing your real sending services.
  2. Enable DKIM signing at each service.
  3. Publish DMARC, starting at p=none with a reporting address.
  4. Review reports, then move to quarantine and reject once legitimate mail passes.

A hypothetical illustration

Picture a dental office, Brightwater Dental (brightwater.example), whose patients receive appointment reminders. A stranger sends a message that copies the reminder's look and says "Pay your balance here." It looks identical to the real one because the stranger copied it. If Brightwater had an enforced DMARC policy for its domain, many receiving providers would refuse a fake claiming that exact domain. If the stranger uses a lookalike domain instead, the policy does not apply. This scenario is invented.

Why training alone is not enough

It is tempting to say "just teach people to spot fakes." Training helps, but it has limits. People are busy, messages are convincing, and a good fake is designed to pass a quick glance. Asking every employee to inspect headers on every message is not realistic.

That is why the sensible approach has layers: domain settings that let receivers block fakes of your exact domain, account protections like multi-factor sign-in, and a few firm habits for the situations that matter most, such as payments and password requests. Each layer catches what the others miss.

A note on AI-written fakes

Writing quality used to be a giveaway: odd grammar, clumsy phrasing. That is a less reliable clue now, because anyone can produce clean, natural text. Do not assume a polished message is genuine. Judge by the request (money, secrecy, urgency) and by verification through a second channel, not by how well it is written.

Key takeaways

  • Names, logos and signatures are all copyable.
  • Real evidence lives in message headers and domain policy.
  • Phone apps hide addresses; tap to expand the sender.
  • A published, enforced DMARC policy gives receivers a reason to block fakes of your exact domain.
  • Lookalike domains need separate watchfulness.

Frequently asked questions

Can the logo and signature be copied?

Yes. Anyone can copy images and text from your website or past emails. A convincing look says nothing about who actually sent the message.

Where can I see who really sent an email?

Most mail apps have a 'show original' or 'view message source' option. It reveals the full headers, including authentication results.

Does a padlock or 'verified' badge prove it is real?

Not by itself. A padlock refers to a web connection, not an email sender. Some mail providers show sender indicators, but they are not available for every domain.

Why do some fakes land in spam and others do not?

Receivers weigh many signals. A domain that publishes strict DMARC gives them a stronger reason to block fakes of that domain.
