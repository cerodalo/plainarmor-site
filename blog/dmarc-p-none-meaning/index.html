What does DMARC p=none mean?

TL;DR: p=none tells receivers to take no specific action on mail that fails DMARC. It turns on reporting, which is useful, but it does not ask anyone to block fake mail. It is the first stage of a rollout, not the finish.

What the setting says

In a DMARC record, p is the policy for your domain. RFC 7489 defines three values. For none, the domain owner requests no specific action. Receivers will usually deliver the message as they normally would, which may mean the inbox, or it may mean their own spam filter catches it for other reasons.

Example:

v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.com

Read it as: "Do not treat failing mail differently because of me. But send me reports."

Why it exists

You need data before you can block anything safely. Most businesses have more sending tools than they remember. p=none lets you see who is sending as your domain, and whether they pass, without risking your own mail.

That is useful, and it is the right first step.

Why it does not stop fake email

A scammer who sends mail pretending to be you will fail SPF and DKIM. DMARC will register a failure. Your policy says none. The receiver is not asked to act, so the message may land in an inbox. Some receivers filter it for other reasons, such as content or reputation, but your domain has not asked them to.

So with p=none:

  • Your domain receives reports about fakes.
  • Fakes are not blocked because of DMARC.
  • Customers might still receive them.

What p=none is good for

  • Discovery. Find all senders.
  • Meeting minimum requirements. Google's sender guidelines for bulk senders state that the DMARC enforcement policy can be set to none. Yahoo's published sender requirements also name p=none as the minimum for bulk senders. (Those rules apply to high-volume senders; see our guide on them.)
  • A safe baseline before tightening.

What it is not good for

  • Stopping someone from using your domain in a fake invoice.
  • Showing customers that you take spoofing seriously. They cannot see your policy, but the receivers do.

How to tell if you are stuck on it

Look up _dmarc.yourdomain.com. If you see p=none and you set it up more than a few months ago, ask:

  1. Are reports arriving, and is anyone reading them?
  2. Do you know which senders pass and which fail?
  3. Is there a plan and date to move up?

Many domains publish p=none and never revisit it. That is common and fixable.

How to move past it

  1. Read reports for several weeks.
  2. Fix legitimate failing senders by adding DKIM or SPF at each vendor.
  3. Move to p=quarantine with a small pct, for example:
v=DMARC1; p=quarantine; pct=10; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.com
  1. Raise pct over weeks.
  2. Move to p=reject the same gradual way once quarantine is quiet.

Our staged rollout guide has the details.

A hypothetical example

A landscaping company at terrafirma.example set p=none two years ago after reading an article. Nobody has looked at reports since. When someone checks, the reports show a printer service and a payroll tool both failing. They fix both and begin the move to quarantine. This is an invented scenario, but the pattern is typical: set it and forget it.

Common questions about timing

You may want to stay at p=none longer if:

  • You have many tools or several departments sending mail.
  • You send seasonal mail, such as year-end statements.
  • You share a domain with a parent company or franchise.

You can move faster if you have a simple setup, such as one mailbox provider and nothing else, but even then, check reports before tightening.

Reading reports at p=none: a quick start

The value of p=none comes from the reports, so it helps to know what you are looking for. Each report lists sending sources and what happened to their mail. Ask three questions:

  1. Which sources send the most mail as us? These should be tools you recognize, like your mailbox provider.
  2. Which known sources are failing? These are fixes to make before enforcement.
  3. Which sources do we not recognize? Investigate. They may be forgotten tools or someone faking your domain.

Do not worry if the raw reports look intimidating. They are machine-readable files, and a report viewer, free or paid, turns them into a table. What matters is that someone looks, regularly, and writes down what they find.

One more reason not to linger

A policy of none can create a false sense of progress. Because a DMARC record exists, a quick check may look reassuring, and a form that asks "is DMARC set up?" can truthfully be answered yes. But the protection against exact-domain faking only starts at quarantine or reject. Put a date in the calendar for your next policy review so the record does not sit untouched for years.

Key takeaways

  • p=none means "take no specific action"; it is monitoring.
  • It gives reports but does not ask receivers to block fakes.
  • It is a fine first step and a poor long-term place to stay.
  • Use the reports to fix senders, then move to quarantine and reject in stages.
  • A set-and-forget p=none is common; review yours.

Frequently asked questions

Is p=none better than nothing?

Yes, because it gives you reports and meets some senders' minimum requirements. But it does not ask receivers to block spoofed mail, so it is a starting point, not protection.

Do Google and Yahoo accept p=none?

For bulk senders, Google's sender guidelines say the DMARC enforcement policy can be set to none, so p=none satisfies that DMARC requirement.

How long should I stay on p=none?

Long enough to see all legitimate senders in reports, often several weeks. Staying on it indefinitely leaves your domain open to spoofing.

What is the next step?

Fix legitimate senders that fail, then move to p=quarantine, ideally with a low pct, and later to p=reject.
