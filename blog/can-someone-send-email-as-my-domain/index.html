Can someone send email as my domain?

TL;DR: Yes, in many cases. If your domain has no DMARC record, or one set to p=none, nothing tells receiving servers to block messages that fake your address. Whether a particular fake lands in an inbox varies by receiver, but you should assume it can.

The honest answer

Email does not verify the From line by itself. A stranger can type your address and send from their own server. What happens next depends on two things:

  1. What your domain publishes in DNS (SPF, DKIM, DMARC).
  2. What the receiving mail provider decides to do.

If your DNS says "block anything that fails," most major receivers will act on it. If it says nothing, or says "just monitor," the receiver is on its own. Some will filter the message as suspicious. Others will deliver it.

Three setups you might have

No DMARC record

Receivers get no instruction from you. This is common for small businesses that set up email years ago and never revisited DNS.

DMARC with p=none

You have a record, but the policy is "take no action." It is a useful first step because it can send you reports. On its own, it does not stop fake mail. See our explainer on what p=none means.

DMARC with p=quarantine or p=reject

You are asking receivers to send failing mail to spam, or to refuse it. This is the setting that works against exact-domain spoofing. It is also the one that can block your own mail if a legitimate sender was forgotten, which is why it is rolled out in stages.

A quick way to look

You do not need special software. A free DMARC lookup tool or a DNS lookup of _dmarc.yourdomain.com (a TXT record) shows what you publish. Compare it with these examples. The domain example.com is a placeholder.

(no record found)                       -> nothing is telling receivers what to do
v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc@example.com   -> monitoring only
v=DMARC1; p=quarantine; rua=mailto:dmarc@example.com   -> enforcing, softer
v=DMARC1; p=reject; rua=mailto:dmarc@example.com       -> enforcing, strictest

Why you will not see it happen

Spoofed messages are sent from outside your systems. They do not appear in your Sent folder, and your provider will not alert you. Typically the first sign is a confused customer saying "I got an odd email from you." Once DMARC reporting is on, you can also see failing messages in aggregate reports.

Hypothetical example

Imagine a small accounting office at fieldstoneaccounting.example. It uses a major email provider and has never touched DNS. A stranger sends a client a message that appears to come from the office manager, asking the client to confirm a new wire address. The client replies to the real-looking thread, and the stranger receives the answer.

Nothing was hacked. The office simply had no published policy, so nothing stood in the way. This is a scenario we made up, but the pattern is a familiar one.

What it will not do

Even with a strict policy, two attacks remain:

  • Lookalike domains, such as a swapped letter in the name. These are different domains, so your policy does not apply.
  • Hacked mailboxes, where the attacker really does log in as you.

So the fix reduces one significant risk. It does not make you immune. Pair it with multi-factor sign-in and a payment-change confirmation habit.

What to do now

  1. Look up your DMARC record.
  2. If missing or p=none, plan a staged rollout.
  3. List every service that sends mail as you: your mailbox provider, invoicing tool, newsletter, CRM, website forms.
  4. Make sure each one is authenticated.
  5. Move to quarantine, then reject once reports look clean.

Common misconceptions

"My provider handles it." Your mailbox provider protects your accounts and servers. Your domain's published policy is a separate setting that lives in DNS, and unless someone set it up, it may be missing or in monitoring mode.

"Nobody would bother with a small company." Impersonation does not require a special target. If your customers trust messages from your domain and pay invoices from them, the domain has value to someone trying to borrow that trust. We are not saying you are being targeted; we are saying the open door is the same regardless.

"I would have heard about it." Not necessarily. Customers may delete a strange message, or reply to the scammer without telling you. The lack of complaints is weak evidence.

"A spam filter fixes this." Filters help your own inbox. They do not change what a stranger can send to your customers in your name.

"I need expensive software." Checking is free. The first step, publishing a monitoring record, is a one-line DNS change. The larger cost is the careful work of finding your senders and tightening in stages.

Key takeaways

  • Without an enforced DMARC policy, a stranger can often send mail that looks like yours.
  • Having p=none is monitoring, not protection.
  • You will not see spoofed mail in your own Sent folder.
  • DMARC does not stop lookalike domains or hacked accounts.
  • Roll out in stages so you do not block legitimate mail.

Frequently asked questions

Can anyone really type my address in the From line?

Yes. The From line is text the sender controls. Whether the message reaches an inbox depends on the receiver's checks and on the policy your domain publishes.

If my email is on Google or Microsoft, am I safe?

Not automatically. Those providers secure your mailboxes, but your domain's DNS still needs its own SPF, DKIM and DMARC settings to tell the world how to treat fakes.

Will the fake email show up in my Sent folder?

No. A spoofed message never touches your mail system, so you will not see it in Sent. You usually learn about it from a recipient.

Does a DMARC record guarantee no one can fake my email?

No. An enforced policy greatly reduces exact-domain spoofing at receivers that honor it, but it cannot stop lookalike domains or hacked mailboxes, and no setting is a guarantee.
