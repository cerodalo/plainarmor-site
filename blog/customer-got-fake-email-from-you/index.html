What to do if a customer got a fake email from you

TL;DR: Stay calm, get a copy of the fake with its full headers, figure out whether it came from a faked address, a lookalike domain or a hacked mailbox, warn the affected people briefly, and then fix the gap. The cause determines the cure, so diagnose before you change anything.

First hour

1. Thank the person and ask for the message

Ask the customer to forward the email as an attachment, not as a plain forward, so the headers survive. If they cannot, ask for a screenshot and the full sender address. Tell them not to click links or reply.

2. Do not panic-reply to the sender

Do not engage with the scammer. Do not click anything in the message.

3. If money or logins are involved, act now

  • If a payment went to the wrong account, call the customer's bank and yours right away and ask about a recall.
  • If a login was entered on a fake page, that customer should change the password and use their provider's account recovery steps.

Work out what kind of fake it is

Look at the real sender address and the headers.

What you findLikely causeWhere to go next
Your exact domain in the From line, but sent from elsewhereSpoofingPublish and enforce DMARC
A similar but different domainLookalikeWarn customers; monitor
It was sent from your real mailboxAccount compromiseSecure the account immediately

For the headers, "Show original" or "View message source" in most mail apps shows authentication results. You are looking for SPF, DKIM and DMARC results for the domain in the From line.

If your own mailbox was involved

  • Change the password and turn on multi-factor sign-in.
  • Check the mailbox for forwarding rules you did not create.
  • Check recent sign-in activity in your mail admin console.
  • Sign out other sessions.
  • Review which other accounts shared the same password.

Do this before anything else, because an attacker with access can see your replies.

If it was a spoofed From line

Your mailbox is probably fine. The gap is in your DNS.

  1. Look up your DMARC record (a TXT record at _dmarc.yourdomain.com). Missing or p=none means receivers were not told to block it.
  2. List every real sender for your domain.
  3. Make sure SPF and DKIM cover them.
  4. Publish DMARC with reporting, then move to enforcement in stages.

A starter record, with placeholders:

v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.com

Do not jump to p=reject immediately. If an invoicing tool or newsletter service is missing from your setup, a strict policy could block your own legitimate mail.

Tell people, briefly

Draft a short notice. Plain, calm, no blame. Here is a sample you can adapt:

We recently learned that a fraudulent email was sent that looked like it came from our company. If you received a message asking you to change payment details, click a link, or share information, please do not act on it. Call us at the number on our website to confirm. We never change bank details by email.

Send it to people who received the fake or are likely to, and post it on your website. Check with an attorney if you think notification laws apply; this article is not legal advice.

Report it

  • IC3 (ic3.gov) for internet-enabled fraud.
  • FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov for scams.
  • Your bank if money is involved.
  • Your email provider's abuse channel for the sending service.

Keep notes

Write down who reported it, when, the sender address, any amounts, and what you did. If you carry cyber insurance, ask your agent whether and when to notify.

After the dust settles

  • Add a line to invoices: "We never change bank details by email."
  • Set up a payment-change callback rule for your own staff.
  • Check DMARC reports each month for a while.
  • Consider a periodic re-check of your domain's settings.

What to say to the customer who reported it

How you respond matters as much as what you fix. The customer did you a favor by telling you.

  • Thank them first. They could have deleted the message and said nothing.
  • Confirm facts, not guesses. Say "We did not send that message" only after checking. If you are still looking, say you are looking.
  • Give one clear instruction. "Do not click or reply. If you have already, tell us."
  • Offer a way to verify you. A phone number on your website that they can use to confirm any request that appears to come from you.
  • Follow up once you know more, even briefly. People remember being kept informed.

Avoid blaming the customer, technical jargon, and long apologies. Calm and specific is what builds trust back.

Key takeaways

  • Get the full original message and headers first.
  • Decide: spoofed, lookalike, or hacked mailbox. Each has a different fix.
  • Secure accounts immediately if there is any sign of takeover.
  • Warn affected customers with a short, calm, factual note.
  • Close the gap with SPF, DKIM and a staged DMARC rollout.

Frequently asked questions

Does a fake email mean my account was hacked?

Not necessarily. It may be a spoofed From line, a lookalike domain, or a hacked mailbox. Checking the message headers and your account activity will tell you which.

Should I contact all my customers?

If the fake asked for money or credentials, a short, calm notice to affected or likely-affected contacts is reasonable. Avoid alarming language and say what to do.

Where do I report it?

Report fraud to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center at ic3.gov, and to your bank if money moved. The FTC also takes scam reports at reportfraud.ftc.gov.

Can I stop it from happening again?

You can make exact-domain faking much harder with enforced DMARC. You cannot prevent lookalike domains or guarantee that no one will try.
