CEO and boss impersonation emails: how to spot and stop them

By The PlainArmor Team · Last reviewed October 3, 2026 · 5 min read

TL;DR: In a boss impersonation scam, an employee receives a message that appears to be from the owner or a manager, asking for an urgent payment, gift cards or private data. The tell is usually pressure and secrecy. A rule that money and data requests are confirmed by a second channel stops most of it.

What it looks like

Here is a hypothetical example. A five-person design studio, Larkspur Design (larkspur.example), has an office manager named Dana. She receives this:

From: "Sam Larkspur" <sam.larkspur@larkspur-mail.example> Subject: Quick favor

Are you at your desk? I'm in a meeting and can't talk. I need you to buy some gift cards for a client today. I'll reimburse you. Keep this between us.

Several things stand out, even though the display name is right: the domain is not the company's, the tone is unusual, the request is urgent, it asks for secrecy, and it avoids a phone call.

This is an invented scenario, not a real case.

Why it works

  • Authority. People respond to the boss.
  • Urgency. "Right now" leaves no time to think.
  • Isolation. "Keep this between us" removes the chance of a second opinion.
  • Small asks first. A first harmless request ("Are you at your desk?") builds a sense that the conversation is real.

Scammers often gather the owner's name and staff names from a company website or public profiles. This is general guidance; we are not claiming a specific rate or trend.

How the fake gets sent

Same three routes as other business email compromise:

  1. Display-name trick. The message shows the boss's name but a different address. Many mail apps show only the name, especially on phones.
  2. Lookalike domain. An address on a domain that resembles yours.
  3. Spoofed or hacked. A faked From line using your real domain, or a genuinely hacked account.

The display-name trick needs no technical skill at all, which is why checking the full address matters.

Habits that defuse it

  1. Look at the full address. On a phone, tap the sender name to expand it.
  2. Reply to confirm, but by a new message to the address you already have, or call.
  3. Make a standing rule: no gift cards, wires or payroll changes on an email request alone.
  4. Give staff permission to say no. A line from the owner: "If I ask for something urgent by email, check with me by phone. I will never be upset."
  5. Do not forward private data on request without confirming.
  6. Tell everyone when a fake arrives. Share the message internally so others recognize it.

What your domain can do

If the fake uses your exact domain, SPF, DKIM and DMARC can help. A DMARC policy of quarantine or reject asks receivers to treat failing messages with suspicion. Receivers decide what to do and results vary, but enforcement is the strongest signal your domain can send.

A sample DMARC record (placeholder domain):

v=DMARC1; p=quarantine; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.com

Do not jump straight there. Start with p=none, read the reports, make sure your real senders pass, then tighten. Otherwise your own legitimate email might be affected.

DMARC does not cover the display-name trick or a different domain. Staff habits do.

What to do if someone fell for it

  • Stop further payments and contact your bank at once about recalling the transfer.
  • Report to the FBI's IC3 at ic3.gov.
  • Change passwords and check mailbox rules if there is any chance an account was accessed.
  • Brief the team without blame.
  • For legal or insurance questions, talk to an attorney or your insurance agent.

A short script for the owner to share with staff

The most useful thing an owner can do costs nothing: say it out loud. Here is a version you can adapt for a team meeting or a short email.

"From time to time someone may send a message that looks like it is from me and asks for something unusual: money, gift cards, passwords, employee or client information. I will never ask for these by email alone. If it feels urgent, call me, or walk over. If I do not answer, wait. You will never be in trouble for checking."

Pair it with a few concrete rules:

  • Name the unusual requests that always need a second check: gift cards, wires, payroll changes, tax forms and client lists.
  • Name a backup person to ask when the owner is traveling or in meetings, since that is exactly when scammers write.
  • Keep a short list of real contact numbers for the owner and key managers, stored somewhere that is not email.
  • Run a ten-minute refresher once or twice a year. Share a recent example, real or labelled as hypothetical, and ask what people would do.

Small teams have an advantage here. Everyone knows the owner's real voice and habits, so something that feels off often is.

Key takeaways

  • The scam relies on authority, urgency and secrecy.
  • The sender name can be faked even when the address is different.
  • A written rule that money requests need second-channel confirmation is your best defense.
  • DMARC helps with exact-domain fakes but not look-alike domains.
  • Roll out DMARC in stages to protect your own mail.

Frequently asked questions

Why do scammers pretend to be the owner?

Staff are used to following the owner's requests quickly. A message that appears to come from the boss, with urgency, can short-circuit normal checks.

How can I tell a real message from the boss from a fake?

Check the full address, not just the display name, and confirm unusual money or data requests through a second channel such as a call or in-person.

Will DMARC stop these?

It can block messages that fake your exact domain at receivers that enforce it. It will not stop a fake using a different but similar-looking domain, or a message sent from a hacked account.

Should we have a policy about this?

Yes. A one-page rule that no payments, gift cards or data transfers happen on an email request alone is easy to write and effective.
