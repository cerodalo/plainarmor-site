How to find out who sends email as your domain

TL;DR: Make a list from three sources: what you know, what your SPF record already names, and what DMARC reports reveal over several weeks. Anything that sends as @yourdomain and is not on the list will break when you enforce DMARC, so this step matters more than any DNS edit.

Why this is the real work

Writing a DMARC record takes a minute. Knowing who sends as you takes weeks, because email comes from more places than people remember. If you enforce before you know, a vendor's invoice email can land in spam and nobody notices until a customer calls.

Source 1: Ask people

Spend fifteen minutes asking office staff, your bookkeeper and your web person. Prompt with categories:

  • Mailbox provider: Google Workspace, Microsoft 365, or another host.
  • Invoicing and accounting: QuickBooks-style tools, billing portals, payment links.
  • Online store and checkout.
  • Newsletter and marketing platforms.
  • CRM and appointment reminders.
  • Website contact forms and WordPress plugins.
  • Support desk or ticketing systems.
  • Scanners, printers and alarm systems that email alerts.
  • Payroll, HR and e-signature tools.
  • Phone systems that email voicemails.

Write down each tool, what it sends, and who owns the account.

Source 2: Read your SPF record

Your SPF record already lists some senders. Look it up as described in our domain-check guide. For example:

v=SPF1 include:_spf.google.com include:servers.mcsv.net -all

Each include: points to a provider. Names can hint at who they are, but SPF is often out of date: old tools linger and new ones are missing. Treat it as a clue, not the answer.

Source 3: DMARC aggregate reports

Publish a monitoring-only record with a report address:

v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.com

Receivers such as large mailbox providers will send daily XML summaries. Under RFC 7489 these list, for each sending IP address, how many messages it sent claiming your domain and whether SPF and DKIM passed and aligned.

Raw reports are hard to read. Options:

  • A report-processing service that turns XML into a table. Many exist at various price levels.
  • A mailbox you read yourself. Practical only for very low volume.

Collect for at least a few weeks. A billing system that sends quarterly will only show up if you are patient.

Reading a report in plain English

You will see rows like: an IP address, a count, and pass/fail columns for SPF and DKIM. Sort them into three buckets:

  1. Known and passing. Good. Nothing to do.
  2. Known but failing. Legitimate sender that needs fixing: add it to SPF or turn on DKIM at the service.
  3. Unknown. Investigate. It could be a forgotten tool, or it could be someone faking your domain. A lookup of the IP's owner often helps.

The third bucket is the useful one. Fakes show up there, too.

A worked, hypothetical example

A small property management office at harborview.example finds this after three weeks:

Rent payment portalsomefailfailLegitimate. Needs setup.

Unknown overseas serverfewfailfailLikely a fake. Will be blocked by enforcement.

The office now has a plan: set up the portal, enable DKIM on the newsletter tool, and keep watching the unknown one. This table is invented.

Fixing each kind of sender

For each legitimate sender, the vendor usually offers a "domain authentication" or "sender verification" page. You publish the DNS records they give you, which typically include DKIM CNAMEs and sometimes an SPF include. Follow the vendor's own instructions, because details differ.

Avoid adding every IP address you see to SPF. SPF has a limit of ten DNS lookups under RFC 7208, and too many includes will make it fail.

Keep the list alive

  • Add a line to your onboarding checklist: "new tool that sends email? Add to the sender list."
  • Review it twice a year.
  • Keep one named owner.

Easy-to-miss senders

These are the ones that most often surprise people when reports come in:

  • A former web developer's contact form that still emails from info@yourdomain.
  • An old CRM trial that someone connected to your domain and never turned off.
  • Accounting or payroll providers that send pay stubs or statements once a quarter.
  • Marketing agencies sending on your behalf under your name.
  • Franchise or partner systems that use your domain for co-branded mail.
  • Calendar and e-signature tools, which send notices that look like they come from your staff.

When you find one, decide: keep it and authenticate it, or shut it off. Both are fine answers. The only wrong answer is not knowing.

Key takeaways

  • Ask people, read SPF, then let DMARC reports reveal the rest.
  • Run monitoring for several weeks, including a full billing cycle.
  • Sort senders into passing, failing but legitimate, and unknown.
  • Fix legitimate failures at the vendor, not by guessing IP addresses.
  • Keep the list updated as tools change.

Frequently asked questions

Why do I need a list of senders?

Enforcing DMARC blocks or quarantines mail that fails authentication. Any legitimate sender you forgot would be affected, so you need to find them first.

What are DMARC aggregate reports?

Daily XML summaries sent by receiving providers to the address in your rua tag, listing sending sources and whether they passed SPF, DKIM and DMARC.

Are the reports readable?

They are raw XML, which is hard to read. Free and paid tools turn them into tables. A small business typically does not parse them by hand.

How long should I collect reports?

Several weeks, ideally covering a full billing and newsletter cycle, so monthly and quarterly senders show up.
