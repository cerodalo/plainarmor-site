What DMARC does not protect against

TL;DR: DMARC helps stop other people from sending mail that fakes your exact domain. It does not stop lookalike domains, hacked mailboxes, scam emails sent to you, malware, or receivers that ignore your policy. Treat it as one important layer.

What DMARC is good at

DMARC lets you publish a rule: "Mail that claims to be from my domain must pass SPF or DKIM aligned with that domain. If it does not, please quarantine or reject it." Under RFC 7489, receivers can then act on that rule and send you reports.

That is a specific, valuable job. Here is what falls outside it.

1. Lookalike domains

If someone registers yourcompany-billing.example and sends mail from it, DMARC for yourcompany.example does not apply. The attacker owns the other domain and can configure valid records for it. See our lookalike article for the full difference.

What helps: staff and customer habits, registering obvious variants, monitoring for new lookalikes, and a payment-verification routine.

2. Hacked mailboxes

If an attacker logs in as a real employee, mail is sent from your real servers with real signatures. DMARC passes because the mail is authentic in a technical sense.

What helps: multi-factor authentication, strong unique passwords, reviewing forwarding rules and sign-in alerts, and prompt offboarding of former staff.

3. Display-name tricks

A message can show "Pat from Acme" as the name while using an unrelated address. DMARC looks at the address domain, not at what name is displayed. If that unrelated domain has no DMARC problem, nothing fails.

What helps: teaching staff to expand the sender and read the address, especially on phones.

4. Scams sent to you

DMARC protects other people from fakes of your domain. It does not protect your inbox from fakes of someone else's domain, unless that other domain has a policy and your provider enforces it. Your own mail provider's filters and settings handle most of the inbound side.

What helps: your provider's spam and phishing protections, user training, and a rule that payment changes are verified by phone.

5. Malware and bad links

DMARC says nothing about what is inside a message. A properly authenticated message can still carry a harmful link or attachment, for example from a compromised account.

What helps: endpoint protection, software updates, link caution, and attachment filtering.

6. Receivers that ignore your policy

DMARC works through voluntary compliance by receivers. RFC 7489 frames the policy as the domain owner's request. Large mailbox providers generally honor it, but some systems do not, and others apply local overrides such as allow lists.

What helps: accepting that no setting is a guarantee, and keeping the other layers in place.

7. Forwarding and mailing-list quirks

Forwarded mail can break SPF, and mailing lists can break DKIM by editing the message. This does not fail DMARC for your mail every time, but it can cause legitimate mail to fail in odd cases. Having DKIM on your own domain helps.

8. Legitimate senders you forgot

A strict policy applied to an incomplete setup can block your own invoices or newsletters. This is a limitation of the rollout, not of DMARC itself, and the answer is a staged approach. See our guide on moving from monitor to enforcement.

A hypothetical example

A small architecture firm at stonebridge.example enforces p=reject. A stranger registers stonebridge-projects.example and sends a client a fake invoice. DMARC for the real domain is irrelevant. The client, following the firm's printed instruction that bank details never change by email, phones to confirm and avoids the loss. The firm's habits did the work that DMARC could not. This scenario is invented.

So is it worth it?

Yes, as a layer. Blocking exact-domain spoofing removes one of the easiest paths for impersonators and is inexpensive compared with the harm a fake invoice can do. But it is one of several controls, and no combination is a guarantee.

Setting honest expectations

When you tell customers or staff about a security improvement, resist the urge to oversell. "We have taken a step that makes it harder for others to send fake emails using our exact domain" is accurate. "Our email is now secure" is not, and it can lead people to relax habits that still matter.

A good internal message is: "We improved our email settings. Please keep verifying payment changes by phone, and keep reporting anything odd."

Key takeaways

  • DMARC covers faking of your exact domain, not lookalikes.
  • Hacked mailboxes pass DMARC because the mail is genuinely yours.
  • Display-name tricks and inbound scams need training and provider filters.
  • Receivers decide; DMARC is a request.
  • Use it as one layer alongside MFA and payment verification.

Frequently asked questions

If I have p=reject, can I stop worrying?

No. Reject helps against faking of your exact domain at receivers that honor it. Lookalikes, hacked accounts and scams aimed at your staff still need other defenses.

Does DMARC filter spam sent to me?

No. DMARC is about mail claiming to be from your domain. Filtering what arrives in your inbox is done by your mail provider's security settings.

Does DMARC encrypt email?

No. DMARC checks sender authenticity. It does not encrypt the contents or hide them from anyone.

Why still bother?

Because exact-domain spoofing is one of the easiest tricks to carry out and one of the cheapest to close off. It is one useful layer among several.
