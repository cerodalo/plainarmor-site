DMARC policies explained: none, quarantine, reject

TL;DR: none means monitor only, quarantine means treat failing mail as suspicious, and reject means refuse it. Reject protects your exact domain best but can block your own mail if a sender is missing, so businesses usually reach it in stages.

The three policies

The p= tag in your DMARC record states what you want receivers to do with mail that claims to be from your domain but fails DMARC. RFC 7489 defines three values.

p=none

No specific action requested. Receivers deliver the message as normal and send you reports if you asked for them. Good for discovery. Does not discourage fakes. See our p=none guide.

p=quarantine

Receivers are asked to treat failing mail as suspicious. In practice that usually means the spam or junk folder, though receivers decide how. Google's help describes quarantined messages as being marked as spam and delivered to recipients' spam folders.

p=reject

Receivers are asked to refuse failing mail during the delivery process. Google's help says the receiving server typically sends a bounce message back. The message does not reach the recipient.

Which one should you use?

Most small businesses should aim for reject and get there in stages:

  1. none while you learn who sends mail.
  2. quarantine once legitimate senders pass.
  3. reject once quarantine has been quiet.

Some businesses stop at quarantine because they have complex sender setups. That is better than none, and it is a reasonable place to stay until you are confident.

The tags that shape a rollout

  • pct: applies the policy to part of failing mail. Example: p=quarantine; pct=25.
  • sp: policy for subdomains. If absent, they inherit p.
  • rua: aggregate report address.

A record that gradually ramps:

v=DMARC1; p=quarantine; pct=25; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.com

Why not jump straight to reject?

Because it cannot tell your forgotten invoicing system from a scammer. Both fail DMARC. The difference is that you can fix the invoicing system, if you know about it. Reports at none or low-pct quarantine reveal it before real damage.

What the policies do not do

  • They apply to your exact domain, not to lookalikes.
  • They do not stop a hacked mailbox from sending real mail.
  • They depend on receivers honoring them.
  • They do not guarantee that no fake gets through.

A hypothetical example

A nonprofit at riverlight.example publishes p=none, then p=quarantine; pct=20 after reports look clean. A donor-receipt tool, used only at year-end, turns out to be failing. At 20 percent, few receipts go to spam, and the staff notice the issue, fix the tool's DKIM, and continue ramping. At p=reject from day one, receipts would have bounced. This scenario is invented.

How receivers treat these in practice

Major mailbox providers generally follow DMARC policies, but smaller or older systems may not. Some also deliver to spam even when you ask for rejection, or accept messages you asked them to block, because of local rules such as allow lists. That is why we say "asks."

Choosing a stopping point

Not every business needs to reach reject on day one, or even in the first year. A sensible way to think about it:

Stay at quarantine for a while if you have many senders, send seasonal mail, or manage several subdomains. Quarantined mail lands in spam, where a person can still find it, so mistakes are recoverable.

Move to reject when several weeks of reports show your legitimate mail passing, no staff or customers report missing mail, and you have a process for adding new senders safely.

Do not stay at none forever. It is the only one of the three that does not ask receivers to treat fakes differently.

Whichever you choose, write down the date, the reason and who owns the next review. Policies get stale when nobody remembers why they were set.

Also keep in mind what happens with subdomains. If you never publish sp=, subdomains inherit your main policy. If a subdomain sends mail you have not reviewed, check it before you move up.

What your staff and customers will notice

At none, nobody notices anything. At quarantine, a customer might mention that your message went to spam, which is a useful signal that a sender needs fixing. At reject, a failing message does not arrive at all, and a sender may receive a bounce. That is why it is worth telling your team in advance when you change a policy, and asking them to report missing mail promptly.

Key takeaways

  • none monitors, quarantine flags, reject refuses.
  • Reject is strongest for your exact domain but needs complete sender coverage.
  • Ramp up with pct and watch reports.
  • Policies are requests; receivers decide.
  • None of them address lookalikes or hacked mailboxes.

Frequently asked questions

Which policy is best?

p=reject gives the strongest protection for your exact domain, but only when your legitimate senders pass. Most businesses reach it through quarantine in stages.

Will receivers always follow my policy?

Not always. RFC 7489 describes the policy as a request, and receivers may apply their own judgment. Major providers generally honor it, but results vary.

What is the difference between quarantine and reject?

Quarantine asks receivers to treat failing mail as suspicious, often routing it to spam. Reject asks them to refuse it during delivery.

Can I mix policies?

Yes, in a way. You can set a different policy for subdomains with sp=, and use pct to apply a policy to part of your failing mail.
