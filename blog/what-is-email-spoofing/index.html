What is email spoofing, in plain English?

TL;DR: Email spoofing is when someone sends a message with a "From" address that is not really theirs. The original email system never checked who was allowed to use an address, so the trick is easy unless a domain publishes rules (SPF, DKIM and DMARC) that tell receivers what to do with fakes.

The short version

Every email has a "From" line. That line is just text the sender typed in. Nothing in the original design of email checked it, the same way nothing stops you from writing any return address on the back of a paper envelope.

Spoofing is using that gap. A stranger types accounts@yourcompany.com into the From field of a message they send from somewhere else. If the receiving mail server has no reason to doubt it, the message lands in the inbox looking like it came from you.

How the post office comparison helps

Think of a letter. The envelope has a return address, and inside is a letter. Anyone can write any return address on the envelope. The post office delivers it anyway.

Email works the same way. Later, the industry added three checks to compare the claimed sender with reality:

  • SPF lists which servers may send mail for a domain.
  • DKIM adds a digital signature that proves the message was not altered and came from a key the domain owns.
  • DMARC tells receiving servers what to do when those checks fail, and asks them to send reports back to the domain owner.

These checks only help if the domain owner publishes them and sets a firm policy. A domain with no policy is like a return address nobody is guarding.

What a spoofed email can look like

Here is a hypothetical example. A small plumbing company, Riverbend Plumbing, uses the domain riverbendplumbing.example. A stranger sends its customer an email with the From line billing@riverbendplumbing.example. The message says the company has changed banks and asks the customer to pay the next invoice to a new account.

The customer sees the real company name and a familiar address. Nothing looks wrong. That is the point of spoofing: it borrows trust that you spent years earning.

Spoofing is not the same as hacking

People often assume a fake email means their account was broken into. Usually it does not. Two different things can happen:

  • Spoofing: the message is sent from outside, with a faked From line. Your mailbox is untouched.
  • Account takeover: someone logs in to a real mailbox and sends real messages from it.

The fixes differ. Email authentication addresses spoofing. Strong passwords and multi-factor sign-in address takeover. You need both, and neither replaces the other.

Why small businesses are affected

Large companies often have a policy in place already. Many small businesses never set one up, because email "just worked" from day one. Recipients cannot tell the difference between a real message and a fake one by looking at it. They are relying on your domain's published settings, whether you set them up or not.

A well-known fraud pattern, called business email compromise, depends on this. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center describes it as a scam aimed at businesses and individuals who make legitimate funds transfers. Spoofing a trusted address is one way to carry it out.

What stops it

You cannot stop a stranger from typing your address into a From line. What you can do is publish rules so receivers reject or quarantine messages that do not pass.

  1. Make sure SPF lists your real senders.
  2. Turn on DKIM for each service that sends as you.
  3. Publish a DMARC record, starting in monitoring mode.
  4. Read the reports, fix legitimate senders that fail, then move to enforcement.

Do not skip straight to the strictest setting. A strict policy can block your own invoices if a sender was forgotten. The staged approach is covered in our guide to moving DMARC from monitor to enforcement.

What this does not fix

DMARC protects your exact domain. It does not stop someone from registering riverbendplumbng.example (a lookalike) or from using a hacked mailbox. We cover both in other guides. Think of DMARC as one strong lock, not the whole building.

Terms you will see, in one place

  • Domain: the part of an address after the @, such as example.com.
  • From line: the sender text shown in your mail app.
  • Envelope sender: a hidden address used between servers during delivery.
  • Header: technical information attached to every email, including authentication results.
  • DNS: the public directory where your domain publishes settings such as SPF, DKIM and DMARC.
  • Policy: your instruction to receivers about what to do with mail that fails checks.
  • Receiver: the mail system that gets the message, such as a customer's mailbox provider.

You do not need to memorize these. They will come up in the other guides, and each is explained where it appears.

Key takeaways

  • Spoofing means faking the From line; it does not require access to your account.
  • Email was built without sender checks; SPF, DKIM and DMARC were added later.
  • Only a published, enforced DMARC policy tells receivers what to do with fakes.
  • Spoofing differs from hacked mailboxes and from lookalike domains.
  • Roll out changes in stages so you do not block your own mail.

Frequently asked questions

Is email spoofing the same as phishing?

No. Spoofing is a method: faking who a message appears to be from. Phishing is a goal: tricking someone into clicking, paying or sharing information. Scammers often use spoofing to make phishing more believable.

Can spoofing happen without my email account being hacked?

Yes. Spoofing does not need access to your mailbox. It only needs the sending address to be accepted by the receiving mail server, which depends on how your domain is set up.

How do I know if my domain can be spoofed?

Look up your domain's DMARC record. If it is missing or set to p=none, receivers are not told to block messages that fail checks. Our guide on checking your domain walks through it.

Does spoofing mean my computer has a virus?

Not necessarily. Spoofing happens on the sender's side, outside your systems. Your own devices and mailboxes can be perfectly healthy.
