Medical practices: fake vendor and payroll emails that look like yours

A medical practice has two kinds of email traffic that matter for fraud. One is about patients. The other is about money: suppliers, payroll, billing services, equipment leases, locum agencies.

The fake emails that cost real money usually target the second kind. They are dull on purpose. A routine reminder, a polite request, a change that sounds administrative.

How it can look in a medical office

Hypothetical scenario one: your payroll contact receives a message that appears to come from a nurse. "I got a new bank, can you update my direct deposit before Friday?" The name is real. The address might be a lookalike, or, if your domain has no protection, it might be an exact copy of a staff address.

Scenario two: the practice administrator receives an email that appears to come from a physician-owner traveling to a conference. "Please pay the attached equipment invoice and don't call me, I am presenting." The sender line carries the practice's domain.

Scenario three: a medical supplier's invoice arrives with updated payment instructions, pointing to a new account. This one may come from the supplier's genuine compromised mailbox, so your own settings do not stop it. A call-back habit does.

Why practices are targeted

Practices run lean. A small administrative team handles many vendors. Physicians are busy and often not at their desk, which makes urgent requests feel normal. Staff names are easy to find. Recurring monthly invoices set a rhythm, so a changed detail slips in.

Also, people tend to answer quickly when email appears to come from a doctor.

What closes the gap

By default, email does not verify the "From" line. DMARC is a public instruction on your domain that tells receiving systems what to do with mail that claims to be from you but does not pass your identity checks. The choices are monitor, quarantine, or reject.

What you do:

  • At your email provider, such as Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, enable DKIM signing and review who is allowed to send as you.
  • At your domain host, publish SPF and DMARC records.
  • Roll out slowly. Begin in monitor mode and read the reports. Your billing service, patient reminder system and e-fax tool may all send as your domain. Add them, then tighten.

What it does not cover

  • Lookalike domains.
  • Hacked mailboxes, whether yours or a vendor's.
  • Texts, calls and fake web pages.
  • Stored data. This is not a replacement for safeguarding records.

Be careful not to treat it as a security program on its own. It addresses one trick: pretending to be your exact domain.

Who owns this in a busy office

In many practices, nobody owns the domain. It was registered years ago by a web designer, the email was set up by a consultant, and the password lives in someone's old inbox. Before anything else, find out who can log in. Put the logins in a shared password manager that the administrator and the physician-owner can both reach. That small step makes every other fix possible.

What to do this week

  • Name a person responsible for the domain and email settings.
  • Check your DMARC status.
  • List all systems that send email as your practice.
  • Enable DKIM, check SPF, publish DMARC in monitor mode.
  • Adopt this rule: any change to a vendor's bank account or an employee's direct deposit gets a phone call to a known number first.
  • Tell physicians that urgent payment requests "from them" will always be confirmed by voice.
  • Turn on two-step sign-in for all mailboxes.
  • Ask your compliance advisor whether any of this should be part of your written policies.

Frequently asked questions

What kinds of fake emails hit medical offices?

Vendor invoices with changed bank details, payroll direct-deposit change requests, and messages posing as the physician or practice administrator asking for a quick payment.

Does this protect patient records?

No. It limits who can send mail that appears to come from your domain. Records need separate access controls and safeguards.

Is DMARC part of HIPAA?

We do not give legal advice. HIPAA may be relevant to how you handle email, so ask your compliance advisor or counsel.

We use a billing company. Does that change anything?

Their emails may be sent as your domain. List them as a sender before you tighten the policy so legitimate mail keeps flowing.

Who should own this task?

The practice administrator or whoever manages your email and domain account, with your IT provider if you have one.
