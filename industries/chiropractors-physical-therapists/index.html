Chiropractic and physical therapy clinics: protect the name on your emails

If you run a chiropractic or physical therapy clinic, you probably spend your day with patients, not with email settings. Yet your domain quietly does a lot of work. It appears on your reminder emails, your billing messages, your insurance correspondence and your supplier orders.

That makes it a handy thing to imitate. Anyone can write a message and put your address on it unless the receiving system has been told to check.

What an impersonation can look like

Imagine a hypothetical Monday. Your front-desk coordinator receives an email that appears to come from the clinic owner. "I'm with a patient all morning. Please pay this for the equipment rental. Details attached. Keep it between us until I finish." The sender line shows your real domain.

Or picture a patient who gets a message: "Your balance is past due. Click here to pay now to avoid a collections referral." Your clinic's name and address appear at the top. The patient owes nothing, but you will field the angry call, and some patients may click.

A third version: a supply company seems to write to say that their account details changed. The payment goes out on the next order.

Why clinics are picked

Clinics have thin administrative teams. The owner often does the books or a part-timer does. There is rarely a second person who asks "did we confirm this?" Staff are used to quickly following the owner's instructions. And your owner's name, your staff names and your website email are all public.

Patients, meanwhile, are used to receiving messages about balances, insurance and appointments. Fake ones blend in.

What actually closes the gap

DMARC is the standard fix. Think of it as a public note on your domain: "If a message claims to be from us but fails our identity checks, quarantine it or reject it." Large mailbox providers read that note and act on it.

You need to touch two places:

  • Your mail provider (for most clinics, Google Workspace or Microsoft 365): turn on DKIM signing.
  • Your domain host (where your website's address is registered or managed): add the SPF and DMARC records.

Before you enforce, spend a few weeks in monitor mode. Your scheduling, billing and reminder tools may send as your domain, and each needs to be accounted for. Once reports show that all real mail passes, tighten to quarantine and then reject.

What it does not cover

  • Lookalike domains, such as a clinic name with a hyphen added.
  • Hacked mailboxes. A stolen password means real mail from a real account.
  • Fraud through other channels like text messages and phone calls.
  • Vendors' own compromised accounts.

That means DMARC is one piece of a routine, not the whole routine.

Front-desk habits count

The front desk sees the most email and has the least time. Give your coordinator explicit permission to say "I will confirm that by phone first," even when the message appears to come from the owner. It helps to tell the owner that this is the plan, so a delayed answer is never held against anyone.

What to do this week

  • Decide who owns the domain and email settings. If it is an outside IT or web person, ask them.
  • Check what your domain currently publishes for DMARC.
  • List every system that sends email as your clinic: reminders, billing, newsletters, forms on your website.
  • Enable DKIM, check SPF, publish DMARC in monitor mode.
  • Create a no-exceptions rule: payment requests and bank changes are verified by phone with the person or vendor, using a number you already have.
  • Tell your team the owner will not ask for urgent payments by email and then go silent.
  • Add two-step sign-in to every mailbox.
  • Put a line on your patient billing emails explaining how you will contact them about payments.

Frequently asked questions

We are a small clinic with two or three staff. Are we a target?

Small size does not protect you. Attackers choose businesses with public contact details and routine payments, and clinics fit that description.

What would a fake email from my clinic say?

Typically a payment request, a change of bank details, or a message to patients about a balance or an appointment link.

Do we have to change how we send reminders?

No, but your reminder and billing software must be listed as authorized senders before you tighten the policy.

Will patients stop receiving our emails?

Not if your real mail passes the checks. That is why the rollout starts in watch-only mode.

Is this a HIPAA issue?

We do not give legal advice. Ask your compliance advisor whether it may be relevant to your policies.
