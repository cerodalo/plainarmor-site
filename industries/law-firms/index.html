Law firm email spoofing: how a fake message gets your name on it

Law firms hold other people's money and other people's secrets. That combination makes a firm's email address valuable to someone who wants to borrow it for an afternoon.

For a small firm, the exposure is specific. Settlement proceeds. Retainer deposits. Trust-account instructions. Closing funds. A message that appears to come from the firm, or from a client talking to the firm, and says "please send it to this account instead" can move real money before anyone notices.

How it can look

A hypothetical case: a client is expecting settlement funds and has been emailing your paralegal for weeks. One morning the paralegal gets a message that appears to be from the client's address. It says the client has closed the old account and attaches new wire details. The tone matches earlier emails, because the sender read the thread.

Now flip it. The client gets a message that appears to be from your firm's domain: "Please send the retainer balance to our updated trust account." The sender line shows your firm's real name and domain. The client has no reason to doubt it.

In both cases the first sign is often a missing payment, days later.

Why small firms are targets

Small firms often have no dedicated IT staff and no second approver for outgoing wires. The work is built on trust and speed, and clients expect fast replies. Firm names and attorney emails are public on websites and bar directories. Attackers can read who handles billing and guess who the paralegal is.

Open settlement or closing dates are a trigger too. Someone who knows money is about to move has a reason to strike now.

What actually closes the gap

Plain email lets the "From" address be set to nearly anything. DMARC is a public instruction on your domain that says: "If a message claims to come from us but fails the identity checks, quarantine or reject it." Large mailbox providers then act on that instruction before the message reaches your client.

The fix has three parts, all configuration:

  1. At your email provider (Microsoft 365, Google Workspace, or a hosted service), turn on signing with DKIM and confirm your sending sources.
  2. At your domain host, where DNS lives, publish the SPF and DMARC records.
  3. Over a few weeks, start in report-only mode, read who sends mail as you, and move to a stricter policy once your real mail, including billing software and newsletter tools, passes.

You do not need new software. You do need access to the two places where these settings live.

What it will not do

  • Lookalike domains. A near-match such as a letter swapped or a hyphen added is a different domain. DMARC cannot control it.
  • Hacked mailboxes. If an attorney's password is stolen, messages from that account are real as far as the checks go.
  • Your client's side. If your client's own email is hijacked, a fake "from the client" message can still reach you.
  • Phone and text. Fraud that skips email is outside DMARC.

A DMARC policy narrows the easy path. It does not replace a call-back rule.

Put it in the engagement letter

A sentence in your engagement letter can set expectations early: the firm will never change payment instructions by email, and the client should call a known number to confirm any request that appears to come from the firm. Clients who have read that line tend to remember it at the moment it matters. Ask your ethics counsel if you want the wording reviewed.

This week

  • Identify who owns the firm's domain registration and DNS, and who administers email.
  • Check whether a DMARC record exists and what policy it sets.
  • Ask your email administrator to enable DKIM signing.
  • Publish or update the DMARC record in report-only mode.
  • Adopt a written rule: no change to wire or trust-account instructions on email alone. Confirm by calling a number already on file.
  • Add a short line to your engagement letters or closing emails telling clients how you will, and will not, communicate payment details.
  • Turn on two-step sign-in for every attorney and staff mailbox.

Frequently asked questions

What is the main email fraud risk for a small law firm?

Money moving through the firm: settlement funds, retainers, trust-account deposits and disbursements. A message that appears to come from your firm, or your client, that changes where money goes is the core scenario.

Can DMARC stop someone emailing my client as me?

It makes it much harder for a stranger to send mail that appears to come from your exact domain and pass the checks at big mailbox providers. It cannot stop lookalike domains or a hacked mailbox.

Do state bar rules require this?

We do not give legal advice. Rules on technology competence and client funds vary by state, so ask your state bar or ethics counsel what may be relevant.

We use a big case-management system. Is our domain already covered?

Not necessarily. Your domain settings are separate from your software. The check is quick and public, so look at your actual domain.

Do we need to tell clients about this?

It helps to tell clients that you will never change payment instructions by email alone and that they should call you to confirm. That habit covers the gaps DMARC cannot.
