Real estate agents: your email address is part of every closing

In a home purchase, one email can matter more than any other. Somewhere in the weeks before closing, a buyer receives instructions for sending a large sum. If that message is fake, the money is usually gone before anyone notices.

Real estate agents are not usually the ones who move the money. But your name and address sit in the middle of the chain. Buyers trust you, they email you with questions, and they treat messages from your domain as reliable.

What it can look like

A hypothetical situation: a buyer is two days from closing. They get an email whose sender line shows your brokerage's real domain and your transaction coordinator's name. It says: "Small update from title. Please disregard the earlier wiring details. Use the account below and confirm once sent." There is a PDF.

The buyer is excited, tired, and busy. They have been told to expect wire instructions. They do not know your coordinator's address has been imitated. The message had no reason to be written inside your systems.

A second pattern targets you. A message that appears to come from a seller, a lender or your own broker asks you to forward "revised payoff information" to the other side. If you forward it, your real address vouches for a fake.

Why agents and brokerages are targets

Deals are public record. Listings, closings and agent names are easy to find. Transactions have deadlines, which makes people hurry. Many small brokerages and solo agents run their own domains with default settings and no IT person.

Closing is also a moment where a change of instructions feels plausible, since many people are involved and details do change.

What closes the gap

DMARC is a public note on your domain that tells receiving mail systems what to do with messages that claim to be from you but fail identity checks. Without it, a receiving system has no instruction. With an enforced policy, big providers can quarantine or reject the fake before the buyer sees it.

The setup:

  1. At your email provider, enable DKIM signing.
  2. At your domain host, publish SPF and DMARC records.
  3. Confirm that your CRM, transaction software and newsletter tools send correctly.
  4. Move the policy from report-only to quarantine to reject over a few weeks.

It is a handful of settings. You do not need to learn email engineering.

What it does not cover

  • Lookalike domains. A buyer might get mail from a name that is one letter off from your brokerage. That domain belongs to someone else, and DMARC cannot touch it.
  • Hacked inboxes. If your password leaks, a message sent from your real account passes.
  • Other parties' email. If the title company or lender is spoofed or hacked, your settings cannot fix it.
  • Texts and calls.

That is why agents should still tell clients about the one rule that works everywhere: confirm wire instructions by phone, using a number from a trusted source, not one in the email.

The first conversation sets the pattern

Tell buyers at the very first meeting how wire instructions will arrive, from whom, and that you will never change them by email. Buyers hear this once, calmly, before the pressure of closing. Repeat it in your closing-week email. It costs nothing, and it works even when the fake comes from a domain you do not control.

What to do this week

  • Check whether your brokerage domain has a DMARC record, and whether it is enforced.
  • Ask your broker or IT contact who controls the domain host.
  • Turn on two-step sign-in for your email.
  • Add a line to your standard buyer and seller email: "We will never change wire instructions by email. Call us before sending funds."
  • Know the title company's phone number and give it to clients up front.
  • List every tool that sends email as you, so nothing breaks when you tighten the policy.

Frequently asked questions

Can someone really send an email that looks like it is from my brokerage?

If your domain has no enforced DMARC protection, a stranger can often send a message with your address in the From line. Some providers will still deliver it.

What is the main scenario for agents?

Altered wire instructions near closing. A buyer gets a message that appears to come from the agent, the title company or the lender, with new account details.

I use a Gmail address, not a domain. Does this apply?

Free Gmail addresses are protected by Google. This applies if you send from your own domain, like yourname-realty.com.

Do I handle wires myself?

Usually the title or escrow company does. But buyers often ask their agent whether instructions are real, so your address is in the middle of it.

Does DMARC stop hacked inboxes?

No. If someone logs into your real mailbox, the mail is genuine to the checks. Use two-step sign-in.
