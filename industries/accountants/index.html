Accounting firms: stop strangers sending email in your name

Accounting firms sit in the middle of other people's money. Clients send bank details, payroll changes and vendor instructions. The firm sends invoices, tax documents and requests for information. Almost all of it travels by email.

That makes a small firm's domain worth borrowing. If a stranger can send a message that looks like it came from your address, they can speak with your authority, and clients are trained to answer you quickly.

What it can look like

A hypothetical example: a bookkeeping client's owner receives an email that appears to be from your firm. It says the monthly payroll funding account has changed and asks for confirmation that they will use the new routing details starting next cycle. The branding is yours, the domain is yours, the language is bland and professional.

Another version points the other way. A message that appears to come from a long-time client, sent to your staff accountant: "Please update my refund to this new account. My old bank closed." It arrives during a busy week, and the client's name is on the message.

Neither message needs access to your systems. They only need your domain to be open to being imitated.

Why firms like yours get picked

Small practices have few layers. One partner and a couple of staff handle everything, and nobody is assigned to double-check payment changes. Seasonal pressure makes shortcuts tempting. Client lists are often guessable from LinkedIn, local business groups and websites.

You also trade in documents that clients expect to receive from you. A fake "your documents are ready" email is familiar enough that people click.

What closes the gap

By default, nothing in email checks whether the sender line is true. DMARC adds that check. It is a short note you publish on your domain telling the world's mail systems: "Messages claiming to be from us must pass our identity checks. If they do not, treat them as suspicious or reject them."

To set it up you work in two places:

  • Your email provider. Turn on DKIM signing and make sure every legitimate sender, such as your practice-management or invoicing software, is accounted for.
  • Your domain host. Add the SPF and DMARC records.

Then you tighten in stages. First you only collect reports. After you see that your own mail passes, you move to quarantine, then to reject. Skipping the first stage is how firms accidentally block their own invoices, so the order matters.

What it does not cover

  • Lookalike domains. A domain with one character off from yours is not yours, and DMARC does not govern it.
  • Compromised mailboxes. If a staff password is stolen, mail sent from that account is genuine to the checks.
  • Clients' hacked accounts. A message from a client's real, but hijacked, mailbox still passes.
  • Voice and text fraud.

So pair it with a firm rule about verifying changes by phone.

Busy season needs a plan made in quiet season

Do the technical work before your peak weeks, not during them. Changes to email settings are easiest when nobody is waiting on a return. Then, when busy season hits, the only thing left to do is follow the verification rule when someone asks to change a bank account.

What to do this week

  • List every tool that sends email for the firm: your main mailbox, billing software, e-signature, newsletters.
  • Find who has the login to your domain host and email admin console.
  • Check for an existing DMARC record.
  • Enable DKIM and publish SPF correctly.
  • Start DMARC in report-only mode and plan a date to tighten.
  • Adopt a rule: bank or payroll detail changes need a call-back to a number already on file.
  • Add a one-line note to your engagement letter explaining that rule to clients.
  • Require two-step sign-in on every mailbox.

Frequently asked questions

Why are CPAs and bookkeepers a target?

They hold client finances, bank details and payroll access, and they act on emailed instructions. A believable message to or from the firm can redirect refunds, payroll or vendor payments.

Is this only a tax-season issue?

Tax season raises volume and urgency, but invoices, payroll changes and vendor bank updates happen all year.

Does DMARC protect client data?

No. It limits who can send mail that appears to come from your domain. Protecting stored client data needs separate safeguards.

We use a client portal. Do we still need this?

Yes, if staff and clients still email each other. Your domain can be spoofed whether or not a portal exists.

Are there FTC rules for tax preparers I should look at?

There may be rules relevant to protecting client information. We do not give legal advice, so ask your compliance advisor what applies to your firm.
