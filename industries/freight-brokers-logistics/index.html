Freight brokers and logistics firms: when a fake rate confirmation looks real

Freight moves on quick decisions. A load posts, a carrier is picked, a rate is confirmed, a truck rolls. Payment follows days or weeks later, and the person who pays is rarely the person who negotiated the rate.

That gap between the work and the payment is where a fake email does its job.

How a fake can look in freight

A hypothetical example: your accounts payable person receives a message that appears to be from a carrier you use every week. "We have switched to a new factoring company. Please send all future payments to the account in the attached notice of assignment." The email thread references a real load number, because load numbers are not secret. The attached form looks professional.

On the shipper's side: a customer receives an invoice that appears to come from your brokerage with a changed remittance account.

A third case: a dispatcher receives what looks like a rate confirmation from a broker, from an address that closely matches the one they know. It sets terms and asks for a quick signature and bank details.

Why brokers and logistics firms are picked

Margins are thin and volume is high, so every payment is routine. Many parties touch each load, and nobody is surprised by a new factoring company or a new contact. Business runs by email and phone, often across time zones and shifts. Staff are heads-down, and an after-hours message from "the carrier" can feel normal.

Load boards, registrations and public directories list company names, contacts and addresses. A stranger can piece together a convincing message from public sources.

What actually closes the gap

Email, by default, does not verify the "From" address. DMARC is a public note on your domain: "If a message says it is from us but fails the identity checks, quarantine or reject it." Large mailbox providers then act on it.

The work is two-part:

  1. At your email provider, enable DKIM signing, and review which services may send as you.
  2. At your domain host, publish SPF and DMARC records.

Brokerages often have many senders: the TMS, accounting, load-board alerts, CRM and marketing tools. List all of them in monitor mode before you enforce. Otherwise a rate confirmation or invoice sent by a tool you forgot may be rejected.

What it does not cover

  • Lookalike domains. In freight these are common, since carriers and brokers deal with new names constantly.
  • Hacked mailboxes. A carrier's genuine inbox may be taken over. Mail from it will pass.
  • Fake carriers and double-brokering. Those are about who is behind the truck, not the sender domain.
  • Phone and fax fraud.

If you remember one thing, make it this: a changed payment detail needs a verified call before money moves.

Shift work and after-hours risk

Freight does not keep office hours. A payment-change notice that arrives at night or on a weekend may be handled by whoever is on duty, who may not know the carrier well. Build the call-back rule into the weekend checklist too, and make it clear that a change can wait until Monday. Waiting a day costs little. Paying the wrong account can cost a great deal more.

What to do this week

  • Find out who controls the domain host and email admin.
  • Check your DMARC status.
  • List all software that sends email as your brokerage.
  • Enable DKIM, correct SPF, publish DMARC in monitor mode.
  • Adopt a rule: payment-account changes require a call to a number from your carrier packet, not from the new email.
  • Add a second approver for any change to remit-to details.
  • Look closely at sender addresses on rate confirmations before signing.
  • Turn on two-step sign-in for all dispatch and accounting mailboxes.

Frequently asked questions

What is the main fraud pattern in freight?

Payment changes. A message appears to come from a carrier asking that payment go to a new account or factoring company, or from a broker asking a carrier to confirm a load with new terms.

Does DMARC stop double-brokering or fake carriers?

No. Those are identity problems with the carrier or load, not with your email domain. Vetting and call-backs are needed.

We send rate confirmations through a TMS. Does that matter?

Yes. Your transportation management system may send as your domain. Authorize it as a sender before you tighten the policy.

Do shippers see our email?

Yes, and a fake from your domain can misdirect a shipper's payment too. Your sender name matters on both sides.

Is it enough to protect only the dispatch address?

Protect the whole domain. Attackers can pick any address at your domain, not only the ones you use.
