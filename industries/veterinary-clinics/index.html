Veterinary clinics: fake invoices and "pay now" emails in your name

A veterinary clinic sends a lot more email than people expect. Appointment reminders. Vaccine notices. Lab results. Orders to drug and food suppliers. Invoices from specialty labs. A single clinic domain carries all of it.

The fraud that matters here is rarely clever. It is a plain message that looks like part of the flow, with one change buried in it.

What it can look like

A hypothetical example: the practice manager gets an invoice email that appears to be from a regular medical supplier. It is a normal-looking invoice, but the bank details at the bottom are different, and there is a note: "We have moved to a new bank. Please use these details from now on." The email thread even quotes a real order.

A different case: a message appears to come from the lead veterinarian, using the clinic's own address. "Need you to buy gift cards for a client appreciation event today. I'll reimburse you. Don't reply, just send the codes." It lands in a technician's inbox during a busy surgery day.

And for pet owners: "Your pet's prescription is on hold. Pay the outstanding balance here." The clinic's logo is attached. The owner worries about the pet and clicks.

Why clinics are picked

Veterinary teams are busy and caring. They respond fast when an animal's care is mentioned. One person often handles purchasing and payments. The vet's name is on the website, and staff names are on social media. Clinics also deal with many vendors, so a new account or "updated" bank detail does not stand out.

What closes the gap

Email lets any sender type any "From" address unless the receiving server is told to check. DMARC is a public note on your domain that explains the checks and says what to do with mail that fails them: monitor, quarantine, or reject. Large mailbox providers use it to decide what to deliver.

Putting it in place involves:

  1. Your mail provider. Turn on DKIM signing.
  2. Your domain host. Publish SPF and DMARC records.
  3. A review period. Watch the reports for a few weeks. Your practice management system, appointment reminder tool, online pharmacy and lab portals may all send mail as your domain. Add them to your SPF record.
  4. Tightening. After the review shows only real mail passing, move toward quarantine and reject.

A common mistake is jumping straight to "reject" and blocking your own reminders. A slower start avoids that.

What it does not cover

  • Lookalike domains. Someone can register something close to your clinic name. DMARC does not cover it.
  • Compromised mailboxes. Yours or a supplier's. Real account, real mail.
  • Fake websites and phone scams.
  • Content of messages. A properly signed mail from your real domain could still be wrong, if someone on your team made a mistake.

So DMARC lowers one risk, and your payment habits cover the rest.

Multi-location and corporate groups

If your clinic belongs to a group or shares systems with sister clinics, check whose domain you send from. A shared domain means a shared policy, and changes should be coordinated. If each location has its own domain, each one needs the check. It is common for one location to be well set up while another still has defaults.

What to do this week

  • Find out who controls your domain registration and DNS.
  • Confirm whether a DMARC record exists.
  • List every service that sends mail from your clinic address.
  • Turn on DKIM and publish DMARC in monitor mode.
  • Set a payment rule: any change in a vendor's bank details is confirmed by calling a number from a previous invoice or contract.
  • Tell your whole team: no gift cards, no secret payments, no urgent transfers requested by email, even from a doctor.
  • Add two-step sign-in to every mailbox.
  • Tell clients in your reminder emails how you handle payments.

Frequently asked questions

Why would a vet clinic be a target?

Clinics buy pharmaceuticals, food and equipment on account, pay a rotating group of vendors and often have a small admin team. That is a good setup for invoice fraud.

Could pet owners get fake emails from us?

Yes, if your domain is unprotected. A message with your clinic's name and address could ask for a payment or a click.

Do corporate-owned clinics need this?

If the clinic sends mail from its own domain, yes. If you use a parent company's domain, ask them.

We use a practice management system for reminders. Does it matter?

Yes. It may send mail as your domain and must be listed as an authorized sender before you tighten the policy.

Is it expensive to set up?

The changes are configuration at your domain host and mail provider. The main cost is time, plus careful testing.
