Can someone email as your dental practice? Here is how to check

A dental practice runs on a steady stream of small payments. Lab bills for crowns and aligners. Supply orders. Equipment leases. Payroll changes. Each one is a chance for a stranger to send a message that looks like it came from you, or from someone you trust.

The most common trick is simple. An email arrives that appears to come from your own domain, or from a vendor you pay, saying "our bank details have changed, please use the account below for the next invoice." If the sender address looks right, the office manager has little reason to doubt it.

What an impersonation can look like in a dental office

Here is a made-up example. Your office manager gets an email at 4:40 on a Thursday. The sender name is the practice owner, and the address ends in your real domain. It says: "I am in a seminar and cannot talk. Please pay the attached lab invoice today and send me the confirmation. New account details are inside."

Nothing about it looks odd. It uses the doctor's name, the real domain, and a task the office manager does every month. That is the whole trick. The message was not written inside your mailbox. It only had to look like it was.

The reverse happens too. A fake message pretending to be from your practice can go out to a patient with a "past due balance" and a payment link. The patient sees your name and your domain.

Why small practices get picked

Practices are busy, front desks are small, and one or two people handle money. There is often no second person who reviews a payment request. Vendors also change, and a "we have a new account" note feels routine.

Your domain is also public. Anyone can see which address your website lists and guess who handles billing. Attackers do not need to break into anything to try this.

What actually closes the gap

Email has a design flaw: the "From" line can be set to almost anything unless the receiving system is told to check. A system called DMARC fixes that. Think of DMARC as a public note on your domain that says, "If a message claims to be from us but fails our checks, treat it this way." The treatments are: just report it, put it in spam, or reject it.

To make it work you set up three things:

  • SPF and DKIM at your email provider (Google Workspace, Microsoft 365, or similar), so your real mail passes the checks.
  • A DMARC record at your domain host, where your DNS settings live.
  • A gradual tightening, starting with "report only," then moving to quarantine and finally reject once you are sure your real mail passes.

None of this changes how your staff write email. It is a few settings in two places.

What it does not cover

Be honest about the limits:

  • Lookalike domains. Someone can register a near-match to your name. DMARC cannot touch a domain you do not own.
  • Hacked mailboxes. If a password is stolen, mail sent from the real account passes every check. Strong passwords and two-step sign-in matter here.
  • Vendors you pay. If a lab's own email is compromised, DMARC on your domain does not help. A call-back habit does.
  • Messages that skip email. Texts and phone calls are separate.

DMARC removes one easy option for impersonators. It is not a guarantee against fraud.

Multiple offices

Dental groups often run several locations on one domain, with one front-desk team per office. Settings apply to the whole domain, so a single change protects every office. It also means one wrong setting can affect every office, which is why the slow, watch-first rollout matters.

What to do this week

  • Find out who controls your domain host and your email provider, and get access.
  • Look up whether your domain already has a DMARC record, and what it says.
  • Turn on SPF and DKIM at your email provider.
  • Publish a DMARC record in report-only mode, then plan to tighten it.
  • Write a rule: no bank detail change is accepted by email alone. Call the vendor on a number you already have.
  • Ask your practice owner to confirm unusual payment requests by voice, even when "traveling."
  • Turn on two-step sign-in for every mailbox.

Frequently asked questions

Why would anyone fake an email from a dental office?

Practices pay lab bills, supply orders, equipment leases and payroll by email-driven routines. A believable message from your address, or to your office manager, is a cheap way to redirect a payment.

Will this protect patient email?

It makes it harder for outsiders to send messages that appear to come from your exact domain, including to patients. It does not secure the mailboxes themselves.

Do I need an IT company to do this?

Often not. The steps are a few settings at your domain host and your email provider. If an IT person manages those, you can hand them the checklist.

Does this stop lookalike domains like yourpract1ce.com?

No. DMARC only covers your exact domain. Lookalikes need other habits, like checking the sender address before paying.

Is DMARC a HIPAA requirement?

We do not give legal advice. Whether it is relevant to your obligations is a question for your compliance advisor.
