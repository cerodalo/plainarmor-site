Staffing and recruiting firms: fake payroll and invoice emails in your name

A staffing agency has two sets of customers: the businesses that hire and the workers who get placed. Both rely on your email. Clients get invoices and timesheets. Workers get offers, schedules and pay information.

Each group trusts messages from your domain, and each presents a different kind of fraud.

How it can look

A hypothetical example on the payroll side: your payroll coordinator receives an email that appears to come from a placed worker. "I changed banks. Please send my next paycheck to this new account." The worker's name is right, and so is the job site, which is on the schedule.

On the client side: a client's accounts payable person receives what looks like an invoice reminder from your billing team. "Our remittance account has changed." The signature is yours.

And on the job-seeker side: someone receives an email "from your recruiter" offering a position, asking for a Social Security number and a bank account "for onboarding." If a stranger can send mail with your exact domain, the offer looks more real.

Why agencies are picked

You handle large amounts of identity and banking information across many people, with frequent turnover. Payroll runs on a schedule, and changes are normal. Recruiters send a high volume of messages, so a fake does not stand out. Job postings and recruiter names are public. Candidates are often eager to respond fast, and clients expect quick billing.

What closes the gap

DMARC is a public instruction on your domain telling receiving mail systems what to do if a message claims to be from you but fails identity checks. Large providers follow it, so a message that looks like it came from your exact domain is more likely to be blocked or sent to spam.

To put it in place:

  • At your email provider, enable DKIM signing.
  • At your domain host, publish SPF and DMARC.
  • List every sender. Staffing firms often use an applicant tracking system, a payroll provider, time-tracking tools, and mass-mail platforms. Check each one.
  • Tighten gradually, starting with monitoring.

A tip for the job-seeker side

DMARC covers your exact domain. To help candidates, say plainly in job postings and offer emails which address your recruiters use and that you never ask for bank details or a Social Security number by email. A candidate who has read that line is harder to fool, even by a fake from a different domain.

What it does not cover

  • Lookalike domains and free-mail addresses pretending to be recruiters.
  • Compromised mailboxes at your agency or at a client.
  • Fake job postings on other sites.
  • Worker-side phone and text scams.

DMARC will not stop a stranger from using your agency's name in a fake job post. It only protects the domain.

Payroll changes deserve friction

A little friction on direct-deposit changes is a feature. Many agencies route those changes through a worker portal with its own login, rather than accepting them by email. If you cannot do that, call the worker on the number in their file before the next pay run. Workers rarely mind a quick check, and it protects them as much as you.

What to do this week

  • Identify who controls the domain host and email admin settings.
  • Check your DMARC status.
  • Inventory every system sending email as your agency.
  • Enable DKIM, correct SPF, publish DMARC in monitor mode.
  • Require a call-back, or a login to your own portal, to change direct-deposit details. Email alone is not enough.
  • Add a "we will never" line to job postings and offer emails.
  • Verify changes to client remittance details by phone.
  • Turn on two-step sign-in for recruiters and payroll staff.

Frequently asked questions

What fake emails target staffing agencies?

Direct-deposit change requests posing as workers, invoice changes posing as the agency to clients, and fake job offers that use your name to collect personal data.

Could scammers use our name in fake job offers?

They may try. DMARC makes it harder to send mail from your exact domain, but fake offers from lookalike domains or free addresses are outside it.

We use an applicant tracking system. Is that a problem?

It likely sends as your domain. Authorize it as a sender before tightening the policy.

Do temps count as employees for email security?

Temps who use your domain should have two-step sign-in. Those who do not use it are not covered by your settings.

Who should own this?

Usually the operations or IT lead, working with whoever manages your domain account.
