Small manufacturers: fake POs and supplier payment changes with your name on them

A small manufacturer trades on reliability. Orders come in, goods ship, invoices go out, and raw material suppliers get paid. Most shops mix modern systems with plain email, and the emails are where the gaps live.

Two kinds of fake matter. One persuades you to ship something or pay someone. The other uses your name to persuade your customers to pay the wrong account.

How it can look

A hypothetical example: your sales coordinator receives a purchase order that appears to come from a known customer's purchasing department. It is a large order with net-60 terms, and delivery is to a new address "because of a warehouse move." The PO number format matches theirs. The message uses the customer's real domain, because the sender has found a way to imitate it.

The payment version: your bookkeeper receives a note from your steel or resin supplier. "We have changed banks. Please use the account below starting with the next invoice."

And the outbound version: a customer receives an invoice that appears to come from your company, with remittance details that are not yours.

Why small shops are picked

Orders and invoices are large and recurring. Teams are lean, so one person might handle quoting, shipping paperwork and accounts payable. You work with suppliers who occasionally do change banks or factoring arrangements, which is a believable excuse. Company names, officers and product lines are easy to find online.

Industrial buyers and sellers also tend to rely on long-standing relationships and quick responses, which makes unusual requests easy to wave through.

What closes the gap

By default, email does not verify the "From" address. DMARC is a public note on your domain telling receiving systems what to do with messages that claim to be from you but fail identity checks. Large providers use it to decide whether to deliver, quarantine or reject.

What it takes:

  • At your email provider, enable DKIM signing.
  • At your domain host, publish SPF and DMARC records.
  • Authorize your senders: ERP, shipping software, quoting tools and any bulk mail system.
  • Tighten in stages, starting by only watching reports.

Check whether any older systems, like a shop-floor server that emails shipping notices, send mail as your domain. Those are the ones most likely to be forgotten.

What it does not cover

  • Lookalike domains that imitate your company or a customer.
  • Hacked mailboxes at your company, a supplier or a customer.
  • Fake purchase orders from new buyers who never claimed to be anyone you know.
  • Phone fraud.

The last one matters in your industry. A new buyer asking for goods on credit is a credit-check issue, not an email-domain issue.

Purchasing and shipping are different jobs

The person who accepts a new order and the person who pulls the freight are often different people. Use that. A simple rule such as "no shipment to a new address without a call to the buyer's known number" costs a few minutes and protects you from fake orders that no email setting can detect. Likewise, the person who receives a supplier's bank change should not be the only one who can act on it.

What to do this week

  • Find out who controls the domain host and the email admin settings.
  • Check your DMARC status.
  • List every system that sends email as your company, including old ones on the shop floor.
  • Enable DKIM, correct SPF, publish DMARC in monitor mode.
  • Verify any bank change by calling a number from your supplier file.
  • Require a call-back and a credit check for large orders from new or changed contacts.
  • Put a line on invoices: "Our remittance details do not change by email."
  • Turn on two-step sign-in on all mailboxes.

Frequently asked questions

What fake emails do manufacturers face?

Fake purchase orders that ask you to ship goods, supplier notes with changed bank details, and fake invoices that appear to come from you to a customer.

Our ERP sends the POs and invoices. Does that matter?

Yes. It probably sends as your domain and must be authorized before you tighten the policy.

Do we need this if most orders come through EDI or a portal?

Less of your traffic is exposed, but remaining email, such as invoices, quotes and payment changes, still carries your name.

Can a fake PO hurt us even if no money moves?

Yes. Goods can ship against an order that never came from the customer. A call-back or portal-only rule for new buyers helps.

Does DMARC secure our plant systems?

No. It deals only with email sender identity. Plant and network security are separate.
