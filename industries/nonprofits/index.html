Nonprofits: protect donor and vendor emails from fakes that use your name

Nonprofits run on trust, and often on thin staffing. A development director, an office manager and a bookkeeper may cover everything. Money arrives as gifts, grants and event proceeds, and leaves as payments to vendors, staff and programs.

If someone can send mail that appears to be from your organization, they borrow a reputation that took years to build.

How it can look

A hypothetical example: a major donor is planning to give by wire. A message arrives that seems to come from your executive director: "We are changing banks this quarter. Please send your gift to the new account below." The donor trusts the director and the message uses the organization's domain.

Another: your bookkeeper receives a note that appears to come from the board treasurer. "I need you to pay this consulting invoice today, off cycle. I will explain later." The treasurer's name is on your website.

A third: a message carrying the nonprofit's name tells supporters that a matching gift deadline is approaching and links to a fake donation page.

Why nonprofits are picked

Boards, staff and donors are publicly listed, often with titles. Programs rely on goodwill, and staff are encouraged to be responsive and warm. Large gifts, grant payments and event revenue arrive in bursts. Small organizations may have no IT person, relying on a volunteer or a part-time contractor. Email settings are often set up once and forgotten.

Also, donors rarely question an organization they believe in.

What closes the gap

DMARC is a public note on your domain that tells receiving mail systems what to do with messages that claim to be from you but fail identity checks. Large providers use it to decide whether to deliver, quarantine or reject.

The steps:

  1. At your email provider, enable DKIM signing.
  2. At your domain host, publish SPF and DMARC records.
  3. List every sender. Nonprofits usually use a donor database, a mass-mail tool, an event platform and a donation processor. Each may send as your domain.
  4. Move in stages: monitor, then quarantine, then reject.

The sender list is where most nonprofits stumble. Your newsletters and donation receipts matter; if they are not authorized and you enforce too fast, donors may stop receiving them.

What it does not cover

  • Lookalike domains that imitate your name.
  • Hacked mailboxes of staff, board members or volunteers.
  • Fake donation pages reached by social media or search.
  • Phone and text fraud.

DMARC is one layer. Your payment rules are another.

Why this matters for donor trust

A donor who is fooled by a message with your name on it will not blame the stranger who sent it. They will remember that it happened to them with your organization. Protecting your domain is partly about money and partly about the quiet reputation you have built. It is also reasonable to mention to your board that this is a small, low-cost control, and that a written payment rule is part of good financial hygiene.

What to do this week

  • Find out who controls the domain host and the email account settings.
  • Check your DMARC record.
  • List every tool that sends email as your organization.
  • Enable DKIM, correct SPF, publish DMARC in monitor mode.
  • Adopt a two-person rule for any change to bank details and for any off-cycle payment.
  • Give donors a phone number to confirm wire instructions, and publish it on your donation page.
  • Move volunteers and board members who speak for the organization onto organization addresses with two-step sign-in.
  • Add a "we never change bank details by email" line to major-gift and grant communications.

Frequently asked questions

Why would anyone fake a nonprofit's email?

Nonprofits receive grants, large gifts and wires from donors and foundations. A message that appears to come from your director, with changed payment details, can redirect real funds.

Could donors be sent a fake appeal?

If your domain is unprotected, a message carrying your organization's address could invite donations to the wrong place. Donors cannot easily tell.

Do discounted or free email plans for nonprofits support DMARC?

Most major providers support it, but the exact steps depend on your plan. Check your provider's documentation or follow a step-by-step guide.

We have volunteers who send email for us. Does that matter?

Volunteers using personal accounts are outside your domain settings. Consider giving them organization addresses with two-step sign-in.

Does this relate to grant or audit requirements?

We do not give legal advice. Funders may have expectations that could be relevant, so ask your auditor or advisor.
