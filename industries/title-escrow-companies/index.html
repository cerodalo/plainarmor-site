Title and escrow: your domain is the trust anchor, so protect it

Few small businesses are as exposed to email fraud as title and escrow companies. Your work includes telling people exactly where to send very large sums, usually by email, usually close to a deadline.

That makes your domain more than a branding asset. It is how buyers, sellers, agents and lenders decide whether an instruction is real.

What an impersonation looks like

A made-up example: a buyer has received wire instructions from your closing officer by email. The next day, a second message arrives. The sender line shows your real domain and the officer's name. It says: "Our bank flagged an issue with the first account. Please send funds to the account below instead."

The buyer, sensible and slightly nervous, replies to ask whether this is right. The reply goes to an address the sender controls. The answer says yes.

Another example runs the other way. Your disbursement team receives a note that appears to come from a seller, listing a new account for proceeds. The message cites the correct property address, because that is public.

Why small title and escrow firms are targeted

You see money move on schedule. Dates are known. Parties are numerous, so any single change seems plausible. Your officers' names are on public documents. Your instructions go out by email so many times a year that people have a habit of acting on them.

Small shops often rely on an outside IT provider and have never looked at their domain settings. There is no malice in that. It simply has not come up.

What actually closes the gap

Email lets a sender set any "From" address unless the receiving side is told to check. DMARC is a public note on your domain that says: "If a message claims to be from us and fails identity checks, quarantine or reject it." Large mailbox providers follow that guidance.

Making it work takes configuration in two places:

  • Your email provider: DKIM signing turned on, and every sending tool, such as your closing software and notification systems, authorized.
  • Your domain host: SPF and DMARC records published.

Then comes the careful part: start by watching reports for a few weeks, fix anything legitimate that fails, and only then enforce. Escrow firms should do this deliberately, because a blocked closing email is a cost too.

What it does not cover

  • Lookalike domains. A name one character off from yours can be registered by anyone. DMARC does not control it.
  • Hacked mailboxes. If an officer's login is stolen, mail from that account passes every check.
  • Other parties. A spoofed or hacked agent or lender can still send convincing instructions.
  • Phone and text.

DMARC is one layer. The most reliable layer in your industry is a call-back to a verified number before any money moves.

Make the phone call easy

A call-back rule works only if the number is easy to find. Put your direct line near the top of every set of instructions, and tell parties at the first contact that a call is part of your process. When the call is expected, buyers are less likely to feel embarrassed to make it, and a message that says "do not call, just email" becomes a warning sign.

What to do this week

  • Find out who controls your domain host and email administration.
  • Check for an existing DMARC record and its policy.
  • Make a list of every system that sends email as your domain.
  • Turn on DKIM, correct SPF, publish DMARC in report-only mode.
  • Put a notice in every set of wire instructions: "Call us at a number you already trust before sending funds. We never change instructions by email."
  • Give buyers and agents your direct phone number at the very first contact, so a call-back is easy.
  • Require two-step sign-in on all accounts.
  • Review who can approve an outgoing wire and whether a second person confirms.

Frequently asked questions

Why are title and escrow companies such a focus?

They send wire instructions and receive large funds from buyers and lenders. A fake message with altered instructions is the clearest path to diverting money.

Does DMARC prevent wire fraud?

No single setting does. It blocks one route: mail that pretends to be from your exact domain. You still need call-back confirmation and good habits.

Should we publish our wiring instructions on a website?

That is a policy choice. Many firms prefer a secure portal and a phone confirmation step. Ask your advisor or underwriter.

Do RESPA or ALTA guidelines cover this?

We do not give legal advice. There may be industry guidance relevant to your business, so ask your advisor or underwriter.

How quickly can this be set up?

The settings can be made in an afternoon, but tightening the policy safely usually takes a few weeks of watching reports.
