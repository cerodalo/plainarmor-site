General contractors: fake draw requests and invoices that look like yours

Construction is paid in stages. A pay application goes out, an owner or lender reviews it, and a payment follows. Along the way you pay subcontractors, suppliers, rental yards and inspectors. A lot of money moves by email, between people who may not have met in person.

Every one of those emails is a place where a fake can slip in.

How a fake can look on a job

Picture this made-up example. A homeowner client who is paying you in draws receives an email that looks like it came from your office. "Draw 3 invoice attached. Please note our bank has changed, use the new account below." Your logo, your signature block, your domain.

Or turn it around. Your bookkeeper gets a message that appears to come from your concrete supplier: "Please update our remittance details. Attached is the W-9 and bank letter." It is mid-month and an invoice is already due.

A third scenario: a message appears to come from the project owner or lender to your project manager, asking to resend the full pay application "to a different contact on their team."

Why contractors are picked

Jobs involve many parties and fast-moving paperwork, so changes seem ordinary. Payments are large and predictable. Project managers are often in the field, replying on phones between site visits, which makes careful checking harder. Your company name, owner and office staff are public through licenses, bids and permit records.

Small contractors also tend to have grown without ever reviewing email settings.

What closes the gap

By default, a mail server will accept any "From" address. DMARC is a public note on your domain that says: "If a message claims to be from us and fails our identity checks, treat it as suspicious." Receiving systems at the large providers then quarantine or reject it.

What you do:

  • At your email provider, turn on DKIM signing and verify your senders.
  • At your domain host, publish SPF and DMARC records.
  • Account for every tool that sends as you: accounting software, estimating or project-management apps, and e-signature.
  • Tighten in stages, starting with monitoring.

For contractors, the fix is not about software upgrades. It is about a few settings and a few weeks of watching.

A note on the field

Your crew and project managers do not need to learn any of this. The point is that mail they send will keep working, and mail that merely pretends to be from you will be turned away more often. The payment rule, though, belongs to everyone who touches money: office staff, owners and project managers.

What it does not cover

  • Lookalike domains a stranger registers.
  • Hacked mailboxes. If a project manager's phone mail is taken over, messages from it are real.
  • Subs' and suppliers' compromised accounts.
  • Text and phone scams.

Be wary of any claim that a setting alone prevents invoice fraud. It does not.

Lien waivers and payment paperwork

Draw packages usually include lien waivers, W-9s and bank letters. Those documents are exactly the attachments a fake message uses to seem legitimate. Keep the originals in your project file at the start of each job, so a "new" version that arrives later can be compared with the first one and questioned if it differs.

What to do this week

  • Find out who owns your domain and email admin.
  • Check the current DMARC record.
  • List all tools sending email as your company.
  • Enable DKIM, check SPF, publish DMARC in monitor mode.
  • Write a rule: any change to a sub's, supplier's or client's payment details is verified by phone using a number from the contract or W-9.
  • Include a note on every pay application: "Our banking details never change by email."
  • Require two-step sign-in on all company mailboxes.
  • Move field staff off personal email for business.

Frequently asked questions

How does fraud hit contractors?

Usually through payment emails. A fake invoice or draw request from your company, or a fake message from a subcontractor or supplier with new bank details, redirects a payment.

We have many subs and suppliers. Does that make it harder?

Many parties make a changed detail seem normal, which helps the fraudster. A written rule for verifying changes helps more than any setting.

Can a fake email use my exact company address?

If your domain has no enforced DMARC policy, a stranger can often send mail showing your address, and some providers will deliver it.

Do owners and lenders see our email?

Often yes. Draw requests and pay applications go to owners, lenders and title companies by email, so your sender name matters to them.

What if my site manager uses a personal email?

DMARC cannot cover personal addresses. Move business mail to your company domain, with two-step sign-in.
