Marketing agencies and IT providers: your domain is a key to your clients

Agencies and IT providers hold a special kind of trust. Clients give you access to ad accounts, websites, servers and passwords. When you send a message, they assume it is safe to act on it.

A fake email that carries your domain can ask for the very things you are normally allowed to ask for.

How it can look

A hypothetical example: a client's office manager receives an email that appears to be from your support desk. "We detected an issue with your backup. Please reply with the admin password so we can fix it before the weekend." The sender line shows your real domain. The tone matches your usual messages.

A billing version: a client's accounts payable receives your monthly retainer invoice, but the payment details have changed.

An internal version: your finance lead gets a note that appears to be from a founder. "Please pay this software vendor today. I will confirm later."

If your domain is not protected, all three can use your exact address.

Why agencies and IT firms are picked

You sit one step upstream of many companies, so a successful trick pays off several times. Account managers are public on LinkedIn and websites. Billing is recurring, and clients rarely question it. Staff are busy, remote and used to fast approvals. Small agencies often have no dedicated security person, and the owner may also be the IT person.

Agencies also use many SaaS tools that send email on your behalf, making a messy domain configuration more likely.

What actually closes the gap

DMARC is a public note on your domain telling receiving mail systems what to do with messages that claim to be from you but fail identity checks. The options are to watch, quarantine or reject. Large providers use it.

To set it up:

  • At your email provider, enable DKIM signing.
  • At your domain host, publish SPF and DMARC records.
  • List every tool that sends as your domain: project management, CRM, ticketing, invoicing, newsletter, proposal and e-signature tools. Agencies usually have a lot.
  • Tighten in stages.

If you do not manage your own DNS records every day, give the job to one named person. Messy sender lists and an SPF record with too many entries are the common problems.

What it does not cover

  • Lookalike domains that imitate your agency.
  • Hacked mailboxes, including those of clients and contractors.
  • Compromised vendor tools.
  • Voice and text fraud.

For an IT firm, stating this to clients is also a good practice. It shows you know where the limit is.

Practice what you advise

If you manage domains or marketing for clients, one of the first things a careful client may do is look at your own setup. A clean, enforced policy on your domain is a quiet signal of care. A missing one is a small embarrassment, and it also leaves your clients exposed to fakes that use your name. Treat your own domain as a client account that deserves the same checklist.

What to do this week

  • Learn who controls your domain host and email admin settings.
  • Check your DMARC status.
  • Inventory every tool that sends email as your company.
  • Enable DKIM, correct SPF, publish DMARC in monitor mode.
  • Tell clients in writing: "We never ask for passwords by email, and our payment details do not change by email."
  • Set a rule that any bank change is confirmed by phone.
  • Use a password manager and sharing tool for credentials, so nobody emails passwords.
  • Turn on two-step sign-in for everyone, with extra care for admins.

Frequently asked questions

Why does an agency's domain matter to clients?

Clients act on your instructions: pay this invoice, approve this change, share this login. A fake from your domain can steer them wrong, and your reputation takes the hit.

We are an IT company. Isn't this already handled?

Many IT firms secure client domains and overlook their own. It is worth a check of your own domain settings.

Can a fake from our domain target our clients' staff?

Yes. A message that appears to come from your support desk or account manager asking for a password or a payment is a classic pattern.

We use a ticketing system and a CRM. Do they matter?

Yes. They send mail as your domain, so each must be authorized before you tighten the policy.

Is DMARC enough for an MSP?

No. It is one control among several, including strong sign-in, role separation and call-back rules.
