Private schools and tutoring centers: fake tuition emails that use your name

A private school or tutoring center sends email that families act on right away. Tuition due dates. Schedule changes. Field-trip fees. Event payments. Parents know the school's name and the head of school's name, and they answer promptly.

That reflex is exactly what a fake exploits.

How it can look

A hypothetical example: a week before the semester's tuition is due, parents receive an email that appears to come from the business office. "We have moved to a new payment processor. Please use the account details below for this term's payment." The school's logo, the usual sign-off and the school's real domain appear on it.

A staff-facing version: the office manager receives a message that appears to come from the head of school. "Need you to purchase gift cards for the faculty appreciation event today. Will reimburse. Keep this quiet so it is a surprise."

For tutoring centers, a note that appears to come from the center to a parent offers a "discount if you pay for the next ten sessions today" to a different payment link.

Why schools and centers are picked

Families pay in predictable bursts, often in large amounts. Contact lists of parents are widely shared, in class directories, social groups and event signups. Administrators are known by name. Small schools frequently run on a few staff, with one person doing finance, admissions and communications. The cultural norm is to respond helpfully and quickly.

What closes the gap

By default, email does not verify the "From" line. DMARC is a public note on your domain that tells receiving systems what to do with a message that claims to be from you but fails the identity checks. Large providers apply it, so a fake from your exact domain is more likely to be stopped.

The setup:

  • At your email provider (often Google Workspace for Education or Microsoft 365), enable DKIM signing.
  • At your domain host, publish SPF and DMARC records.
  • Check all senders: your student information system, tuition billing, newsletter tool and online forms.
  • Tighten in stages, watching reports first.

Be careful with the timing. Do not tighten the policy in the middle of the tuition cycle; make the changes in a quiet week so you can fix issues without stress.

What it does not cover

  • Lookalike domains that parents might not notice.
  • Hacked mailboxes of staff or teachers.
  • Fake payment pages reached by text or social media.
  • Calls and texts.

Think of DMARC as one useful lock. It will not review a message's content, and it will not prove a payment link is real.

Tell parents once, clearly

Families remember simple rules. At enrollment and again in each tuition notice, say where payments go and that the business office never changes account details by email. Add a phone number that a parent can call. A short, friendly note like this does more for parents than any technical setting, because it works even when the fake comes from a domain you do not own.

What to do this week

  • Find who controls the domain host and who manages email.
  • Check your DMARC status.
  • List all systems that send mail as your school.
  • Enable DKIM, correct SPF, publish DMARC in monitor mode.
  • Tell families in your next tuition notice: "Our payment details do not change by email. Call the business office to confirm."
  • Publish the business office phone number where parents can find it.
  • Require a second approver for any change to bank accounts or payee details.
  • Turn on two-step sign-in for administrators and office staff.

Frequently asked questions

What is the main email risk for a private school?

Tuition and fee payments. A message that appears to come from the business office with changed payment details could redirect a family's payment.

Do parents trust school email by default?

Mostly yes, which is why a convincing fake works. Parents often act quickly on messages about money or student safety.

We use a tuition management service. Does it matter?

It probably sends mail as your domain or its own. Confirm which, and authorize it before tightening your policy.

Does this cover student privacy rules?

We do not give legal advice. Student privacy laws may be relevant to your email practices, so ask your counsel or advisor.

Is a tutoring center too small to matter?

Size does not shield a domain. Any business that bills families by email can be imitated.
