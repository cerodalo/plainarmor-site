Insurance agencies: protect the sender name your clients trust

An insurance agency is built on trust that clients rarely test. They pay premiums when you ask, open policy documents you send, and answer renewal reminders. You are, in a sense, a recognizable voice in their inbox.

That is useful to you and, unfortunately, to anyone who wants to borrow the voice.

What it can look like

Here is a hypothetical. A small-business client gets what appears to be a renewal notice from your agency, sent from your real domain. "Your commercial policy renews in 10 days. Our payment portal has moved. Please pay using the link below." The link leads somewhere that is not yours.

Another case concerns claims. A message appears to come from a client to your account manager: "Please send the claim settlement to my new bank account. My old one was compromised." The client's name and policy number are correct, because they came from a past email or a public record.

A third pattern: a message that appears to be from a carrier representative asks your agency to resend a batch of client documents "for an audit."

Why agencies get targeted

You hold client names, policy numbers, addresses and, often, payment details. You send recurring, predictable messages: renewals, certificates, invoices. Clients are used to acting on them quickly, because lapsed coverage is a real worry. Staff turn over, and agencies often add new tools for quoting and e-sign, each sending mail on your behalf.

Small agencies also have little time to review email settings, so defaults stay in place for years.

What closes the gap

By default, mail systems will accept a "From" address without proof. DMARC is a public note on your domain that tells receiving systems what to do when a message claims to be from you but fails identity checks. Options are to watch, to send it to spam, or to reject it.

To set it up:

  • Turn on DKIM signing at your email provider.
  • Publish SPF and DMARC at your domain host.
  • Inventory your senders: your agency management system, quoting tools, e-signature, newsletter and payment reminder systems.
  • Roll out in stages from monitor to quarantine to reject.

If you do this well, a stranger trying to send mail that looks like it came from your exact domain will be much more likely to hit a wall at major mailbox providers.

What it does not cover

  • Lookalike domains, which are separate domains you do not own.
  • Hacked inboxes, at your agency or at a client.
  • Payment fraud that starts elsewhere, like a call or text.
  • Carrier systems, which you do not control.

It is honest to say DMARC reduces one common path. It will not stop every scam that borrows an insurance theme.

Certificates and renewals are the pattern

Agencies send certificates of insurance, binders and renewal notices all the time, often from a shared mailbox. Those shared mailboxes need the same care as personal ones: two-step sign-in, a known list of who can use them, and a clear place in your sender inventory. A shared address that nobody owns is exactly the kind that gets forgotten.

What to do this week

  • Check who holds the logins to your domain host and email admin console.
  • Look up your current DMARC record.
  • List the tools that send mail as your agency.
  • Turn on DKIM; fix SPF; publish DMARC in monitor mode.
  • Add a standard line to renewal and invoice emails: "We will never change payment instructions by email. If in doubt, call us."
  • Make staff confirm any change of bank details on a phone number from the policy file.
  • Require two-step sign-in for every user.
  • Plan a review date to tighten the policy.

Frequently asked questions

What fake emails affect insurance agencies?

Premium payment notices with changed payment instructions, fake policy documents with links, and messages to your staff posing as carriers or clients.

Do carriers cover the agency's domain?

No. Your agency domain is your own to protect. Carrier systems protect their own domains.

Does DMARC protect client data?

No. It only addresses who can send mail that appears to be from your domain.

Do state insurance regulations require this?

We do not give legal advice. Some state regulators have expectations on data security that may be relevant, so ask your compliance advisor.

Can this block our own bulk mailings?

Only if senders are missing from your setup. Review reports first, add each legitimate sender, then tighten.
