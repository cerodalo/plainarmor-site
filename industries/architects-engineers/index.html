Architecture and engineering firms: keep fake emails from borrowing your name

Design firms live in project threads. Drawings go to contractors, RFIs go to owners, invoices go to clients, and consultant bills come back the other way. Most of it moves by email, much with links to large files.

That rhythm gives a fake a lot of cover. Nobody is surprised to get a link from you.

What it can look like

A hypothetical example: a contractor on one of your projects receives an email that appears to come from your project architect. "Revised structural set attached. Open the shared folder here. Sign in with your work email." The link goes to a page that collects the contractor's password.

The money version: a client receives your regular monthly invoice, apparently from your firm, with a different remittance account. Or your accounting person receives a note from a structural consultant: "Our bank info has changed."

The sender line, in each case, looks exactly like yours if your domain is not protected.

Why small design firms are targeted

Your project list is often public: permits, press releases, award notices, portfolio pages. Principals and project managers are named. Clients expect fast turnarounds on drawings and invoices. The practice may involve a mix of staff, freelancers and consultants, each using email in different ways. Few small firms have dedicated IT staff, so defaults tend to stay.

What actually closes the gap

DMARC is a public note on your domain that tells receiving mail systems what to do with mail that claims to be from you but fails identity checks: report, quarantine, or reject. Without it, systems are on their own, and a fake may get through.

The work is at two places:

  • Your email provider: turn on DKIM signing and review who can send as your domain.
  • Your domain host: publish SPF and DMARC records.

Design firms often use many cloud tools: project management, proposal software, billing, newsletters. Add each legitimate one to your authorized list. Do that before you tighten, or the firm's own proposals may go to spam.

Things your team can do that DMARC cannot

DMARC does not look at links. Pick one file-share service for client work and tell your clients and contractors in writing which one it is. A link from any other service becomes a warning sign worth a phone call. It also helps to put project invoices in a portal or a consistent PDF template, so a changed layout is noticeable.

What it does not cover

  • Lookalike domains that you do not own.
  • Hacked mailboxes, including a stolen password on a principal's account.
  • Fake file-share pages reached by other channels.
  • Compromised consultants.

An honest summary: DMARC narrows one entry point. It does not review content.

Project teams change often

Every project brings new contractors, owners and consultants onto your email threads. That constant churn is part of why a single odd message is easy to miss. Keep a short contact list for each project with verified phone numbers, created at kickoff. When something unusual arrives later, you will know where to call.

What to do this week

  • Find who controls the domain host and the email admin console.
  • Check whether DMARC exists and what it is set to.
  • List all tools that send email as your firm.
  • Enable DKIM, correct SPF, publish DMARC in monitor mode.
  • Choose a single file-sharing service for client work and say so in your project kickoff emails.
  • Require phone confirmation of any new bank details from consultants or clients, using a number from the contract.
  • Turn on two-step sign-in for all staff.
  • Make sure freelancers and consultants use a firm address, not personal accounts, when speaking for the firm.

Frequently asked questions

What do fake emails look like for design firms?

Often a drawing or RFI file-share link that appears to come from your firm, or an invoice or consultant payment note with changed bank details.

We exchange big files all the time. Does that raise risk?

It makes link-based fakes more believable, since recipients expect a link from you. Telling clients which file-share you use helps.

Does DMARC stop fake file-share pages?

No. It stops mail that pretends to be from your exact domain. A fake page reached by another route needs other habits.

Do consultants and subconsultants matter?

Yes. They often send you invoices and files. A call-back rule for payment details covers them.

How long does it take?

The settings are quick. Tightening safely takes a few weeks of reading reports.
