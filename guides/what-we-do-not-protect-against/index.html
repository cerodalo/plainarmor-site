What PlainArmor Does Not Protect Against

PlainArmor does one narrow job well: helping stop strangers from sending email that looks like it came from your exact domain. That is worth doing, but it is not everything, and we would rather tell you the limits than let you assume. This guide covers what a strong DMARC setting does not stop, and what else can help.

What our fix does

When you follow Fix it, your domain publishes a DMARC policy asking receiving mail systems to reject messages that fail authentication for it. A stranger who types your exact domain in the From address will find it much harder to get that message delivered. That is the problem we address.

Lookalike domains

A lookalike domain is a different domain that resembles yours, perhaps with a swapped letter or an added word. Someone else owns it, and they can set up email on it, with their own correct authentication.

DMARC on your domain cannot stop this, because it only governs your exact domain. What helps: train staff and customers to read the full sender address; confirm payment or account changes by phone using a number you already have; consider registering obvious variations of your domain; and report abusive domains to the registrar.

Hacked mailboxes

If someone steals a password and signs in to a real mailbox, mail sent from it is genuine mail from your systems. It passes SPF, DKIM and DMARC. This is called account takeover, and email authentication cannot detect it.

What helps: multi-factor sign-in on every mailbox, unique strong passwords, alerts for unusual sign-ins, and checking for unexpected forwarding rules. If you suspect a break-in, change the password and sign out all sessions.

Display-name tricks

Here the sender's visible name matches someone you know, but the address behind it is unrelated, often a free webmail account. Your domain is not involved, so DMARC cannot help. What helps: look at the full address, especially on a phone, and flag outside senders if your mail system supports it.

Phishing to your staff from other domains

DMARC on your domain controls how others treat mail claiming to be from you. It does not filter mail arriving at your staff from elsewhere. A message from another domain, with correct authentication of its own, can still be a scam.

What helps: the filtering built in to your mail provider or a separate email gateway, a habit of verifying unusual requests, and an easy way for staff to report suspicious messages.

Missed senders can block your own mail

This is a limit of the setting itself. A strict policy can cause legitimate mail to be refused if a service that sends as you is not authenticated. The instructions are built around checking that mail still works first, and undoing a change is one step.

What does not change

A simple set of habits covers much of what email authentication cannot: confirm bank-detail changes by phone, require two people for large payments where possible, keep software updated, and make it easy for people to ask when something feels off.

Frequently asked questions

Does DMARC stop lookalike domains? No. They are separate domains owned by someone else.

Does DMARC stop a hacked mailbox? No. That mail is genuine and passes the checks.

Does DMARC stop phishing sent to my staff? Not from other domains.

What helps with the rest? Multi-factor sign-in, strong passwords, mail filtering, phone verification and staff awareness.
