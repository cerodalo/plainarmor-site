DIY, Monitoring or Instructions: How to Choose

By The PlainArmor Team · Last reviewed October 3, 2026 · 4 min read

There are three common ways for a small business to get a strong DMARC policy in place: do it yourself from public documentation, subscribe to a monitoring service, or follow step-by-step instructions. None is right for everyone. This guide compares them neutrally, including where our own option is not the best fit.

Option 1: Do it yourself

You read the documentation from your mail provider and the DMARC standard, publish the records, and manage the process on your own.

Strengths: No extra cost beyond your time. You learn how your email works. Full control.

Tradeoffs: The documentation assumes technical vocabulary. The main risk is moving to a strict policy before every legitimate sender is authenticated, which can cause real mail to be refused. You also need to decide the order of steps, how long to wait, and how to read reports.

Best for: Someone comfortable with DNS who enjoys the learning and has time to do it carefully.

Option 2: A monitoring subscription

A monitoring or report service receives your DMARC reports and presents them as readable dashboards, often with alerts. You still publish records and decide when to tighten the policy, though some services offer guidance.

Strengths: Visibility into who sends as your domain, which is especially valuable when many services do. Ongoing alerts if something changes.

Tradeoffs: It is usually a recurring subscription. It shows you data but may not tell you exactly what to do next in plain language. You still need someone to act on it, and you should understand what the provider does with your report data.

Best for: Businesses with many sending services or a changing email setup, and someone who will look at the dashboard.

Option 3: Follow step-by-step instructions

This is what PlainArmor offers. Fix it is a one-time set of plain-English instructions that you follow yourself, with a fast route for businesses where no other services send as the domain and a careful staged route, with checkpoints at days 7, 14, 21 and 28, where other services do. It always checks that mail still works first, and undoing a change is one step.

Strengths: A clear order of steps, no jargon, a one-time price, and a safety-first sequence. We never touch your DNS, so you keep control.

Tradeoffs: You still do the work yourself. It is not a continuing monitoring dashboard, and it addresses one problem only. The optional quarterly re-test gives later checks, but it is not a full monitoring suite.

Best for: Owners without an IT person who want a defined path and are happy to follow instructions.

How to decide

Ask yourself four questions.

  1. How many services send email as my domain? Few suggests DIY or instructions can work; many suggests monitoring becomes more valuable.
  2. How comfortable am I with DNS? If it makes you nervous, a guided sequence reduces mistakes.
  3. How much time can I give? All options involve some waiting; DIY involves the most planning.
  4. Do I want ongoing visibility? If yes, consider monitoring now or later, alongside any option.

These can be combined. Many owners follow instructions to get to enforcement, and add monitoring later if their email setup grows.

What none of these options solve

No DMARC approach stops lookalike domains, display-name tricks or hacked mailboxes. Those need other measures, such as multi-factor sign-in, mail filtering and phone verification of payment changes. See our guide on what we do not protect against.
