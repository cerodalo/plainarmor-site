DMARC, SPF and DKIM Basics: Plain-English FAQ

By The PlainArmor Team · Last reviewed October 3, 2026

SPF, DKIM and DMARC are three settings stored in your domain's DNS that help receiving mail systems decide whether a message claiming to be from your domain is real. SPF lists the servers allowed to send for you. DKIM adds a verifiable signature to your messages. DMARC connects both to the From address people see and tells receivers whether to ignore, quarantine or reject failures.

For a small business, DMARC is the one that turns the other two into protection, because it carries the instruction. This page explains each in plain English, how they fit together, and where their limits are.

Questions and answers

What are SPF, DKIM and DMARC?

They are three DNS-based settings that help receivers judge whether email claiming to be from your domain is genuine. SPF lists servers allowed to send for you, DKIM adds a digital signature to your messages, and DMARC tells receivers what to do when checks fail.

What is SPF?

SPF, Sender Policy Framework, is a DNS record listing which servers may send email for your domain. A receiving system compares the sending server against that list. SPF checks the hidden envelope sender, not the From address people see, so alone it does not stop spoofing.

What is DKIM?

DKIM, DomainKeys Identified Mail, attaches a cryptographic signature to outgoing messages. The matching public key is published in your DNS, so a receiver can verify the message was authorized by your domain and was not altered in transit. Each sending service usually has its own key.

What is DMARC?

DMARC is a DNS record that ties SPF and DKIM to the From address people actually see and tells receivers what to do with failures: nothing, quarantine, or reject. It also lets you request reports about mail using your domain. It is the policy layer.

Why is DMARC needed if I already have SPF and DKIM?

SPF and DKIM can pass without matching the visible From address, and neither tells receivers to reject failures. DMARC adds the alignment check and the instruction. Without a firm DMARC policy, receivers decide on their own how to treat mail that fails.

What are the DMARC policy options?

There are three: p=none asks receivers to take no action and just report, p=quarantine asks them to treat failing mail as suspicious, usually spam, and p=reject asks them to refuse it. Reject gives the strongest protection against fake mail using your exact domain.

Is p=none enough?

No, not for stopping spoofing. p=none is a monitoring mode that asks receivers to take no action on failing mail, so fakes may still be delivered. It is a safe starting point, but protection comes from moving to quarantine and then reject.

What is alignment?

Alignment means the domain verified by SPF or DKIM matches the domain in the visible From address. DMARC passes only when at least one of them passes and aligns. This is what stops a stranger from passing checks with their own domain while showing yours.

What are DMARC reports?

DMARC reports are summaries that receiving mail providers send to an address you name in your DMARC record. Aggregate reports, called rua, show who is sending mail using your domain and whether it passed. They are XML files that are hard to read raw.

Where do these records live?

They live in your domain's DNS as TXT records. DMARC sits at _dmarc.yourdomain, SPF in a TXT record at the domain itself, and DKIM at a selector name followed by _domainkey. You edit them at your DNS host, which may be your registrar, your website host or a separate DNS service.

Can DMARC break my email?

Yes, if you enforce it before all your legitimate senders are authenticated. A p=reject policy can cause real mail from an unconfigured service to be refused. Starting with monitoring, checking senders, and tightening gradually avoids this, and a rollback is a single change at your DNS host.

Do I need all three?

For DMARC to pass, you need at least one of SPF or DKIM working and aligned; having both is better. In practice, your mail provider usually handles much of this, and the work is publishing DMARC and making sure every sender is covered.

Do these settings protect against phishing in general?

Only partly. They address messages that fake your exact domain. They do not stop lookalike domains, messages from other domains to your staff, or hacked mailboxes. Treat them as one layer among several, alongside multi-factor sign-in, mail filtering and the habit of confirming payment changes by phone.
