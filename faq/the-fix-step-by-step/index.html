The Fix, Step by Step: PlainArmor FAQ

By The PlainArmor Team · Last reviewed October 3, 2026

Fix it is a set of step-by-step instructions that you follow yourself. They take your domain from having no firm email protection to a DMARC policy of p=reject, which asks receiving mail systems to reject messages that falsely use your domain.

There are two routes. The fast route suits businesses where no other services send email as the domain and takes about 20 days to reach the strictest setting. The careful staged route suits businesses where other services send as you, with checkpoints at days 7, 14, 21 and 28, always checking that mail still works first. Undoing any change is one step. Below are the details.

Questions and answers

What do the Fix it instructions have me do?

Fix it walks you through publishing a DMARC record for your domain, then tightening it in stages until it tells receivers to reject fake mail. You do each step yourself at your DNS host. Every step is written in plain English, with the exact value to enter.

What is the fast route?

The fast route is for businesses where no other services send email as their domain. It takes about 20 days to reach the strictest setting, p=reject. It is quicker because there are no outside senders to account for, so there is less that could be missed.

What is the careful staged route?

The staged route is for businesses where other services, such as billing, newsletter or CRM tools, send email as the company. It adds checkpoints at days 7, 14, 21 and 28, and always checks that your mail still works first before tightening anything.

How do I know which route is mine?

If you are unsure whether other services send email as your domain, choose the staged route. The instructions help you find out. Fast is for domains where only your normal mailbox provider sends your mail. Staged is the safer choice whenever there is doubt.

Why does it take weeks and not one afternoon?

A strict setting can block real mail if a legitimate sender was missed, so the process watches for a period before enforcing. That waiting is the safety margin. It gives time to notice a forgotten sender before receivers start rejecting its mail.

Do I need to do this myself?

Yes. We never touch your DNS, so you or someone you trust applies each change. You do not need to be technical, but you do need to log in to where your domain's DNS is managed. If someone else manages it, you can pass the steps to them.

Where do I make the changes?

You make the changes at your DNS host, the service that controls your domain's DNS records. This is often your registrar or your website host. The instructions explain how to find out who your DNS host is if you are not sure.

What are the checkpoints on the staged route?

The checkpoints are planned review points at days 7, 14, 21 and 28. At each one you confirm your legitimate mail is still arriving and check for senders that were missed, before moving to a stricter setting. If something looks wrong, you pause.

What if my email stops working?

Undo the last change. Rollback is one step: you put the earlier setting back at your DNS host, and the instructions show exactly what to restore. Mail normally returns to its previous behavior once the change has spread, which can take a little time.

Will the steps break my email?

They are designed not to. Each stage begins by checking that your mail still works, and strict enforcement comes only after that. The risk is a missed legitimate sender, which is why the staged route exists and why undoing is a single step.

How long do DNS changes take to apply?

DNS changes can take anywhere from minutes to several hours to be seen everywhere, depending on your DNS host and caching. The instructions build in time for this, so you will not be told to move on before a change has had a chance to spread.

What if I get stuck on a step?

Contact support and describe what you see on your screen. Support replies within one business day. We cannot log in to fix it for you, but we can tell you which value or button the step means and what to try next.

What is the end result of following the instructions?

The end state is a DMARC policy of p=reject for your domain, which asks receiving mail systems to reject messages that fail authentication for it. That makes it much harder for strangers to send mail that looks like it is from your exact domain.
